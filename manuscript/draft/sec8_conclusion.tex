We compared a weight-conditioned graph reinforcement learning policy with NSGA-II and four other baselines for metro line extension on two real benchmark grids, at about $10^4$ simulated lines per method and with ten seeds. After correcting the benchmark's demand and fairness measures, NSGA-II gave better deployable trade-off sets than the learned policy on both grids, and the gap in search quality grew from Xi'an to the larger Amsterdam grid. The learned policy returned almost the same line for different weight vectors. In a leave-one-line-out test, NSGA-II and a scalarised genetic algorithm recovered more of the real Xi'an Line~2 than a matched random-search baseline, and the learned policy did not. A warm start from another city helped in one direction in all five seeds and not in the other, which five pairs cannot settle.

For this benchmark and these budgets, evolutionary multi-objective search is the sensible default. Open questions are whether a policy can be made to respond to the weight vector, whether the comparison changes on much larger networks, and whether need-based population groups change the conclusions about fairness. The code, anchors, tuning runs, run records and table scripts are public.
