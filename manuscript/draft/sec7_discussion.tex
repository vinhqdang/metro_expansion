\section{Discussion}\label{sec:discussion}

\paragraph{What the comparison shows.} Under the same budget and the same scoring, NSGA-II gives better deployable trade-off sets than the learned policy on both instances and in both line lengths. The two methods are closer in the search archive on the smaller grid. During training the policy samples many lines from a distribution that is diverse enough to cover the front, but the greedy line it finally deploys for each weight vector is much less varied: its 15 deployable points contain \XianRLLines\ distinct lines on average on Xi'an, against \XianNSGALines\ for NSGA-II, and its three vertex solutions are almost the same line (Section~\ref{sec:res-tradeoffs}). The multi-objective purpose of the policy, one model that answers any weight vector, is therefore not met.

\paragraph{Why the policy ignores the weights.} In exploratory runs on a tuning seed, which are not part of the evaluation, the learned weight embedding contributed little to the move and start decisions compared with the pooled graph embedding, and the trained policy converged to one compromise line. Several remedies had no lasting effect in those short runs: centring the weight vector, an MLP query, weight-gated keys, advantages divided by their within-group standard deviation, many weights with one rollout each, and a sparser weight distribution. What helped was a fixed prior that adds the weight-scalarised one-step gains to the logits of both heads. This prior is a hand-built scalarised greedy rule, and it raised the number of distinct deployable lines but changed expected utility only a little. We therefore regard the weight-conditioned policy as unsolved, not as a finished method with a small flaw. Because the prior shapes what the policy does, the ablation without it is reported next to the full method.

\paragraph{Implications for rail planners.} For a planner who wants a set of alternative extensions that trade demand, equity and accessibility, a standard evolutionary multi-objective search is the more reliable tool in this benchmark and is cheaper to run. A learned policy would be attractive if it delivered many plans for any new weight vector without a new search, or if training on one city shortened the search on another. The first is not delivered here. The second is suggested in one direction only, and our evidence for it is weak (Section~\ref{sec:res-transfer}).

\paragraph{Fairness measures.} The generalised Gini welfare and the worst-served share behave sensibly where a Gini index does not, in particular for lines that serve nobody. They do not remove the problem that the population groups are price bands. The conclusions about equity should be read as conclusions about these groups.
