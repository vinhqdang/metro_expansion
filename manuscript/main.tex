\abstract{The Metro Network Expansion Problem (MNEP) -- deciding where to extend rail lines and place new stations -- has recently been formulated as a sequential decision process solvable by reinforcement learning (RL). Prior work has shown that graph neural network (GNN) state encoders improve demand satisfaction over heuristics, that multi-objective reward decomposition can balance demand against social equity and accessibility, and that tabular RL can match deep RL performance at a fraction of the training cost and carbon footprint -- but no method combines all three, and none has been tested from small emerging systems to the world's largest urban rail networks. Emerging systems pose a further difficulty: with only one or two operating lines, the rail network alone is too sparse to define candidate expansion regions or estimate demand. We propose a GNN-based, multi-objective actor-critic policy for urban rail transit (metro and tram/light-rail) network expansion, scalarizing demand, equity, and accessibility via Tchebycheff decomposition, made sample- and carbon-efficient via cross-city curriculum transfer and prioritized replay with reward shaping. To address data sparsity in emerging systems, each city's existing bus network is used purely as an auxiliary signal -- informing candidate-region definition and demand proxies, never the action space; decisions and the resulting network design remain rail-only throughout. We evaluate across multiple Asian cities spanning roughly an order of magnitude in current rail network scale, anchored by Hanoi and Ho Chi Minh City (Vietnam) and Xi'an and Chengdu (China), extended as further open data is confirmed. \section{Introduction}\label{sec1}

Urban metro systems are among the most capital-intensive pieces of city infrastructure, and decisions about where to extend a network shape a city's mobility, equity, and economic geography for decades. The \emph{Metro Network Expansion Problem} (MNEP) -- selecting which stations or regions to add to an existing metro network under a limited budget -- is a combinatorial, NP-hard sequential decision problem, and is itself a special case of the broader \emph{Transport Network Design Problem} (TNDP) \citep{su2024}. Classical approaches rely on mixed-integer programming or heuristics that require restrictive, expert-defined search-space constraints \citep{zhang2024}.

Recent work has reframed MNEP as a Markov Decision Process solvable by reinforcement learning (RL). \citet{su2024} show that a graph neural network (GNN) state encoder over a heterogeneous region graph substantially improves origin-destination (OD) demand satisfaction over prior heuristics and RL baselines. \citet{zhang2024} instead emphasize the multi-objective nature of expansion decisions, decomposing a reward over OD demand, social equity, and accessibility via Tchebycheff scalarization, but without a GNN state representation. Most recently, \citet{michailidis2026} argue that MNEP's state-action space is small enough that deep RL and GNNs are unnecessary altogether, and show that a tabular RL formulation matches deep RL performance with an order-of-magnitude fewer training episodes and a substantially smaller carbon footprint -- evaluated, however, only on Xi'an and Amsterdam, both modest-scale networks.

All three of these studies treat MNEP purely on the basis of the existing rail network, and all train and evaluate a separate policy per city from scratch. This creates a practical difficulty for emerging metro systems that are still very small: at the time of writing, Hanoi operates only two lines and Ho Chi Minh City only one, giving too few candidate regions and too little usable ridership signal to define a well-posed expansion problem or estimate travel demand directly from rail data alone. We address this purely as a \emph{data} problem, not a scope change to the method: where metro-derived signal is too sparse, we use each city's existing bus network only as an auxiliary input for defining candidate expansion regions and as a demand proxy (Section~\ref{sec:data}). The method's action space, objectives, and object of design remain metro network expansion throughout; no bus-route decisions are made by the policy.

Separately, \citet{kang2019} address coordinating last-train timetables with bus bridging services on an \emph{existing} urban railway network. This is an operational-phase problem, and we treat it as related work rather than a methodological component: it evidences that bus service is planned around rail networks in practice, supporting our choice to draw on bus data as an auxiliary signal, but our contribution does not extend to modeling bus bridging or timetabling.

This paper addresses three open gaps in the rail network expansion literature. First, no existing MNEP method combines a GNN state representation with a multi-objective fairness-aware reward. Second, no study tests whether tabular RL truly remains sufficient at the scale of the world's largest metro networks, nor at the opposite extreme of small, data-sparse emerging systems. Third, all three MNEP papers above train and evaluate a separate policy per city from scratch, leaving open whether learned expansion policies transfer across cities of different scale.

We propose a GNN-based, multi-objective actor-critic policy for metro network expansion, trained with a cross-city curriculum that transfers policy weights from small networks to large ones, combined with prioritized experience replay and reward shaping for sample efficiency. We evaluate the method across multiple Asian cities spanning roughly an order of magnitude in current metro network length (Hanoi's $\sim$21\,km to Chengdu's $\sim$500+\,km), anchored by Hanoi and Ho Chi Minh City (Vietnam) and Xi'an and Chengdu (China), with the final city set extended opportunistically to any additional city for which we can confirm sufficient open network, demand, and population data (Section~\ref{sec:data}).

\section{Related Work}\label{sec:related}

\subsection{Metro network design and expansion}

The Transport Network Design Problem, of which MNEP is a sub-problem, has traditionally been addressed with mixed-integer programming and metaheuristics (genetic algorithms, simulated annealing, tabu search, ant colony optimization), which require restricting the search space with predefined corridors or extensive hand-crafted constraints \citep{su2024}. Framing MNEP as a sequential decision process addressed by RL removes the need for these restrictions by allowing the agent to explore the design space directly.

\subsection{Reinforcement learning for MNEP}

\citet{su2024} (MetroGNN) represent candidate urban regions as a heterogeneous multi-graph with spatial-contiguity and OD-flow edges, and train an attentive actor-critic policy with an action mask enforcing network-design feasibility constraints; the method improves OD demand satisfaction over prior heuristic and RL baselines by 15.9\% on average across their evaluated scenarios (exceeding 30\% in higher-budget/complex cases), but optimizes a single objective (demand) and trains a fresh policy per city.

\citet{zhang2024} formulate MNEP as sequential station selection and use a multi-objective actor-critic framework, integrating Tchebycheff decomposition to combine OD demand, social equity, and radiation accessibility into a single scalarized reward without limiting the search to a fixed weighting. Their method outperforms traditional heuristic algorithms (genetic algorithm, ant colony, mathematical programming) by over 30\% and their own weighted-sum RL variant by a smaller margin, using a real-world Xi'an case study; it does not use a graph-structured state representation.

\citet{michailidis2026} reformulate MNEP as a Non-Markovian Reward Decision Process to shrink the effective state-action space, and show a tabular Monte Carlo RL method with fairness-based reward functions matches deep RL performance while using 18$\times$ fewer training episodes and producing 12$\times$ less estimated CO$_2$, evaluated on Xi'an and Amsterdam.

None of the three reports results outside of a handful of already-mature metro systems, leaving open how well their approaches hold up on small, data-sparse emerging networks or on networks approaching the largest in the world.

\subsection{Related operational-phase work on rail-bus coordination}

\citet{kang2019} address last-train timetabling and bus bridging service coordination on an existing urban railway network via a decomposable mixed-integer linear program, showing that coordinating last-train connections across lines and dispatching bridging buses substantially reduces the number of stranded transfer passengers. This is an operational-phase problem -- it assumes a fixed rail topology and does not address network design -- so we treat it as related work rather than a methodological component of our approach. It does, however, evidence that bus service is planned and evaluated around a city's rail network in practice, which we draw on only to justify using bus data as an auxiliary demand/candidate-region signal (Section~\ref{sec:data}); our method neither designs nor evaluates bus routes.

\subsection{Cross-city curriculum and sample efficiency}

To address the training-cost and carbon-footprint critique of deep RL for MNEP raised by \citet{michailidis2026} without abandoning the GNN state representation, we introduce a cross-city curriculum: policy weights are pretrained on the smaller networks (Hanoi, Xi'an) and used to initialize training on the larger networks (Ho Chi Minh City, Chengdu), rather than training each city's policy from scratch. This is combined with prioritized experience replay over transition batches and potential-based reward shaping, which accelerates credit assignment without altering the optimal policy.

\begin{algorithm}
\caption{Cross-city curriculum training for the GNN multi-objective policy}\label{alg:curriculum}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require Cities ordered by network scale $C_1 \le C_2 \le \dots \le C_n$; Tchebycheff weight set $W$
\State Initialize policy/value network parameters $\theta_0$ randomly
\For{$i = 1, \dots, n$}
    \State Initialize $\theta \gets \theta_{i-1}$ \Comment{transfer weights from the previous, smaller city}
    \For{each training episode on city $C_i$}
        \State Sample Tchebycheff weight vector $w \sim W$
        \State Roll out policy on $C_i$'s region graph, collect transitions $(s_t, a_t, r_t, s_{t+1})$
        \State Store transitions in prioritized replay buffer $\mathcal{D}_i$
        \State Sample prioritized batch from $\mathcal{D}_i$, apply potential-based reward shaping
        \State Update $\theta$ via actor-critic gradient step on the scalarized reward
    \EndFor
    \State $\theta_i \gets \theta$
\EndFor
\State \Return $\theta_n$
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\section{Case Studies and Data}\label{sec:data}

We anchor the evaluation on four cities chosen to span small/emerging to very large/mature metro networks (Table~\ref{tab:cities}), and to allow direct comparison against prior work: Xi'an was previously studied by \citet{michailidis2026}, enabling a direct numerical comparison; Chengdu is one of the largest metro networks in the world and is the home city of this journal's publishing institution; Hanoi and Ho Chi Minh City are, to our knowledge, not previously studied in the MNEP literature. Because MNEP RL methods are trained per city rather than pooled across a fixed benchmark set, the city set is not architecturally fixed at four -- we extend it with additional Asian cities (candidates: Beijing and Changsha, the two cities used by \citet{su2024}, if their released code/data prove usable) wherever open data can be confirmed, since a larger and more diverse city set strengthens both the transfer-learning claim and the scale-dependent generalization claim.

\subsection{Data sources per city}

The sources below have been directly verified by inspecting the underlying repositories (not merely located), except where noted.

\textbf{Network topology and planned extensions.} Existing rail geometry for all four cities is obtainable from OpenStreetMap/Overpass. For Xi'an and Chengdu specifically, a public dataset covering 38 Chinese cities provides \emph{year-by-year} (circa 2010--2019) station-point and line shapefiles, confirmed by direct inspection; because it is multi-year, it additionally enables a validation protocol not used by any of the three reference RL papers -- checking whether the policy's highest-priority predicted extensions, given an early-year network as the starting state, match what was actually built by a later year. Planned-extension maps exist as official planning diagrams for all four cities (Hanoi's 8-line master plan; Ho Chi Minh City's MAUR master plan; Xi'an's and Chengdu's Five-Year-Plan transport documents) but require manual digitization -- none is available as a ready-to-use shapefile.

\textbf{OD demand.} Ho Chi Minh City has by far the strongest confirmed source: the public \texttt{CityScope/CSL\_HCMC} repository (MIT Media Lab) ships real shapefiles -- metro routes/stations, bus routes/stops, a 2018 household-travel-survey OD matrix, district- and ward-level population, points of interest (POI), and ward-level census with age breakdown -- verified by direct inspection, though the repository carries no explicit license, so we treat it as research/fair-use only and do not redistribute the underlying files. For Xi'an, a public MIT-licensed environment (via \citet{michailidis2026}'s code release and its \texttt{mo-tndp} submodule) ships a ready-to-use 29$\times$29-cell grid with 463{,}010 nonzero OD pairs, the existing metro lines as grid-coordinate paths, and per-cell house-price/equity groupings -- confirmed by direct inspection. That dataset's own citation file attributes it to \citet{michailidis2024} rather than to the KDD~2020 paper we had provisionally assumed as its origin; we cite the confirmed source and do not assert the earlier, unverified lineage. Notably, \citet{zhang2024} -- one of our three reference RL baselines -- also uses Xi'an as its case study, so our Xi'an results can be compared against two, not just one, of the three reference methods' originally-reported numbers. For Hanoi and Chengdu (and as a fallback for the others), we construct a gravity-model demand proxy from WorldPop population-density rasters and OpenStreetMap/Amap/Baidu POI density, calibrated against published ridership figures (e.g., Hanoi's Cat Linh--Ha Dong line: 13.68M riders in 2025, up 15.3\% year-on-year; Ho Chi Minh City Line 1: 20.56M riders in 2025).

\textbf{Bus network (auxiliary signal only).} Each city's existing bus route network is used solely as an auxiliary input to the rail expansion problem: it helps define a richer set of candidate expansion regions in cities where the rail network alone is too sparse (Hanoi, Ho Chi Minh City) and contributes an additional demand-proxy feature. Bus routes are never an output or design target of the method. Ho Chi Minh City's bus network is available as a real shapefile (CSL\_HCMC, above); the remaining cities use OpenStreetMap bus-route extraction. \emph{Coverage caveat, confirmed by direct inspection:} CSL\_HCMC's 189 bus stops fall entirely within a small downtown/pilot-study area, giving nonzero bus-derived signal for only $\sim$32 of the city's 322 candidate zones ($\sim$10\%); outside that area the bus feature is uniformly zero, and the OpenStreetMap fallback (or a wider bus dataset, if found) will be needed for full-city coverage.

\textbf{Administrative boundaries / candidate regions.} GADM (the Database of Global Administrative Areas) provides consistent district-level administrative boundaries for all four cities, usable to define MetroGNN-style candidate expansion regions.

\textbf{Coordinate systems.} Chinese POI sources (Amap, Baidu) report coordinates in the obfuscated GCJ-02 system, which must be converted to WGS84 before merging with OSM/metro station coordinates; this conversion step is required for Xi'an and Chengdu but not for the OSM-only Vietnamese cities.
