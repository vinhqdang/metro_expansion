\title{Fair and Transferable Graph Reinforcement Learning for Metro Network Expansion in Asian Cities}

\author{
\name{Quang-Vinh Dang\thanks{CONTACT Quang-Vinh Dang. Email: dqvinh87@gmail.com}}

\maketitle

\begin{abstract}
The Metro Network Expansion Problem (MNEP) is a sequential decision process solvable by reinforcement learning (RL), but no method combines graph neural network (GNN) demand encoding, multi-objective equity-demand decomposition, and sample-efficient tabular RL, or has been tested across networks of very different scales. We propose a GNN-based, multi-objective actor-critic policy for rail transit expansion, scalarized via Tchebycheff decomposition and made sample-efficient via cross-city curriculum transfer, using bus networks only as an auxiliary signal. On Xi'an and Ho Chi Minh City, a five-seed sweep under deterministic training finds no reliable advantage for it over its ablations or a tabular baseline, itself outperformed by a classical genetic algorithm; an encoder ablation collapses to near-zero demand on its irregular graph, and cross-city curriculum transfer yields genuine positive transfer and higher coverage under a reduced training budget. We present this method as an exploratory tool for comparing expansion plans, not a validated decision-support system.
\end{abstract}

\begin{keywords}
metro network expansion; reinforcement learning; graph neural networks; multi-objective optimization; social equity; transfer learning
\end{keywords}

\section{Introduction}\label{sec1}

A metro line is one of the few pieces of infrastructure a city builds that it can never meaningfully undo. Roads can be repaved, buildings retrofitted, bus routes redrawn overnight -- but once tunnels are bored and viaducts poured, a metro network's shape is fixed for the better part of a century, and every household, employer, and land developer within walking distance of a station reorganizes itself around that fact. Get the sequencing right, and a single well-placed extension can unlock accessibility for a district that spent decades cut off from the rest of the city; get it wrong, and the mistake compounds for generations, entrenching exactly the spatial inequalities that transit investment is supposed to correct. This is why the \emph{Metro Network Expansion Problem} (MNEP) -- given an existing network and a limited construction budget, decide which stations or line segments to add next -- is not merely an interesting combinatorial puzzle. It is one of the highest-stakes recurring decisions in urban planning, and it is made, in most of the world, with far less quantitative rigor than the size of the investment would justify.

Part of the reason is that MNEP is genuinely hard. It is a special case of the broader \emph{Transport Network Design Problem} (TNDP), a family of network-design problems that has occupied operations researchers for decades \citep{farahani2013}, and the metro variant inherits the TNDP's combinatorial explosion: for a city with even a modest number of candidate station sites, the number of ways to sequence an extension budget into a connected line is astronomically large, and evaluating any single candidate network requires simulating how demand redistributes itself across the entire city once that network exists. Classical solution methods -- mixed-integer programming, genetic algorithms, simulated annealing, tabu search, ant colony optimization -- have made real progress on TNDP variants, but nearly all of them purchase tractability by first constraining the search space by hand: restricting candidate stations to a handful of pre-drawn corridors, or fixing the number of lines and their rough geometry before optimization even begins \citep{su2024}. The result is that the algorithm is not really choosing where to build; a planner already did, and the algorithm is polishing the choice.

Reinforcement learning (RL) offers a different bargain. Rather than search a pre-constrained space, an RL agent can be set loose on the full combinatorial problem -- select a station, observe how the network's demand coverage changes, select the next station, repeat -- and learn, through repeated simulated construction, which sequences of decisions tend to produce networks that serve the city well. Three recent papers have each taken a piece of this idea and shown that it works. \citet{su2024} (MetroGNN) represent a city as a graph neural network (GNN) sees it: candidate regions are nodes, spatial adjacency and travel-demand flow are edges, and an attentive actor-critic policy learns to walk this graph, extending or starting lines while a feasibility mask keeps the result physically buildable. The GNN state representation is what makes this work at all -- a policy that can see the city's spatial and demand structure, rather than treating each candidate station as an anonymous slot, substantially outperforms prior heuristics and flat RL baselines at satisfying origin-destination (OD) travel demand. \citet{zhang2024} start from a different frustration: demand-maximizing algorithms have a well-documented tendency to route transit toward wealthy, already-well-connected areas, because that is where the largest raw travel flows are, which is precisely the outcome transit equity policy exists to prevent \citep{hansen1959,raza2023}. They reformulate the reward as a vector -- demand, social equity, and a gravity-style accessibility measure derived from the ``radiation model'' of human mobility \citep{simini2012} -- and combine the three via Tchebycheff scalarization \citep{zhangli2007}, a decomposition technique from multi-objective optimization \citep{hayes2022} that lets a single training run explore genuine trade-offs rather than committing to one fixed weighting up front. Most recently, and most provocatively, \citet{michailidis2026} argue that both of the preceding papers are more machinery than the problem needs: they reformulate MNEP as a Non-Markovian Reward Decision Process that shrinks its effective state-action space, and show that a tabular Monte Carlo RL method -- no neural network, no GPU -- matches deep RL's performance while using an order of magnitude fewer training episodes and a correspondingly smaller carbon footprint. It is a genuinely uncomfortable result for anyone building GNN-based MNEP methods, our own effort included, and it deserves to be taken seriously rather than argued around.

Read together, though, these three papers leave a set of gaps that none of them, individually, was positioned to close. First, no existing method combines the spatial sophistication of a GNN state representation with the fairness-aware, multi-objective reward that \citet{zhang2024} show matters for real-world adoption; a planner today must choose between a policy that understands city structure and one that understands equity, not both at once. Second, \citeauthor{michailidis2026}'s efficiency claim -- that deep RL is unnecessary overhead -- was tested on Xi'an and Amsterdam, both mid-sized, data-rich, already-mature metro systems; whether it still holds at the scale of one of the largest metro networks on Earth, where the state-action space genuinely is enormous, is an open empirical question, not a settled one. Third, and perhaps most consequentially for practice, all three papers train and evaluate a freshly initialized policy for each city they study. This is a reasonable simplification for a first paper on a new method, but it leaves unanswered a question that matters enormously for the cities that most need this technology: does anything learned while training on one city's network transfer to another, or must every city's planning authority independently pay the full training cost from scratch?

That third gap is not an abstract concern for us -- it is a practical blocker. Our motivating case studies are Hanoi and Ho Chi Minh City, Vietnam's two largest metropolitan areas, both mid-expansion right now: Hanoi operates two lines, Ho Chi Minh City one, with several more under construction or in planning. Neither city's existing rail network is remotely large enough, on its own, to define the rich candidate-region structure or estimate the OD demand signal that a GNN-based policy needs to learn from. Prior MNEP methods, developed and validated on cities with a decade or more of accumulated metro infrastructure, simply do not have anything to work with here. We treat this as a data problem rather than a reason to change what the method optimizes: where a city's own rail network is too sparse, we let its existing bus network -- typically far denser, since bus networks are cheaper to build and iterate on -- supply an auxiliary signal for candidate-region definition and demand estimation (Section~\ref{sec:data}). At no point does this let the policy choose, evaluate, or optimize a bus route; the action space, the objective, and the artifact the method ultimately designs remain metro network expansion, full stop. Separately, \citet{kang2019} study how last-train timetables and bus bridging services get coordinated on an \emph{existing} urban railway network -- an operational-phase problem, distinct from the design-phase question we address, but one that reinforces the same point from a different angle: bus and rail networks in practice are never planned in isolation from each other, which is exactly why treating bus data as a legitimate input to rail network design, without letting it become a rail network design \emph{output}, is the right scope boundary to draw.

Putting these threads together, we propose a GNN-based, multi-objective actor-critic policy for urban rail transit network expansion that keeps \citeauthor{su2024}'s spatial representation, keeps \citeauthor{zhang2024}'s fairness-aware multi-objective reward, and directly answers \citeauthor{michailidis2026}'s efficiency critique -- not by abandoning the GNN, but by making the deep-RL training itself cheaper through a cross-city curriculum (pretraining on small networks before fine-tuning on large ones, an idea with no precedent in this literature since no prior MNEP paper trains across more than one city) together with prioritized experience replay \citep{schaul2016} and potential-based reward shaping. We evaluate the resulting method on a set of Asian cities chosen specifically to stress-test the parts of the design space the existing literature has not: Hanoi and Ho Chi Minh City at the small, data-sparse end, and Xi'an and Chengdu -- one of the largest metro networks in the world -- at the large, mature end, with the city set left open-ended rather than fixed, since the value of the transfer-learning claim only grows with a larger and more diverse set of training cities. This paper reports results from the first two cities in that programme: a fully implemented, tested, GPU-trained instance of the method on Xi'an, benchmarked directly against a direct execution of \citeauthor{michailidis2026}'s own public tabular-method code release on the same data, together with two ablations that isolate the contribution of the GNN representation and the multi-objective reward respectively; and, on the structurally distinct polygon graph of Ho Chi Minh City, the same ablation design together with the first measured test of the cross-city curriculum itself (Section~\ref{sec:method-curriculum}), warm-starting Ho Chi Minh City's training from Xi'an's trained policy. Chengdu and Hanoi remain ongoing work (Section~\ref{sec:discussion}), with the infrastructure to complete them already built and described in detail so the path from this paper to that fuller result is concrete rather than aspirational. We are explicit, up front, about what this paper's evidentiary status does and does not license: it presents a useful exploratory tool for generating and comparing candidate expansion plans across demand/equity/accessibility trade-offs, not a validated decision-support system ready to replace an existing planning authority's own demand-modeling and cost-estimation process, or to be trusted as the sole basis for an actual construction decision -- a distinction Section~\ref{sec:discussion} returns to directly rather than leaving implicit.

In summary, this paper's contributions are:
\begin{enumerate}
\item A GNN-based, multi-objective actor-critic policy for MNEP that, to our knowledge, is the first to combine a heterogeneous spatial-graph state representation with Tchebycheff-decomposed multi-objective reward over demand, equity, and accessibility, and the first to condition the policy itself on the sampled objective-weight vector so a single trained model spans the full trade-off surface rather than committing to one weighting.
\item A cross-city curriculum and sample-efficiency design (prioritized replay, reward shaping) that responds directly to the training-cost and carbon-footprint critique of GNN-based MNEP methods, without abandoning the GNN.
\item A rail-only scope discipline -- using bus network data purely as an auxiliary state signal, never as an action space -- that lets the method extend to small, data-sparse emerging metro systems that no prior MNEP study has evaluated on, and that a direct with/without ablation on Ho Chi Minh City (Section~\ref{sec:experiments-hcmc}) confirms measurably improves demand satisfaction (5 of 5 seeds, exact sign test $p=0.03$), even though the signal itself is nonzero for only about 10\% of candidate zones.
\item A fully real, end-to-end implementation and experimental results on two structurally distinct cities, Xi'an and Ho Chi Minh City, including five methodological pitfalls discovered only through actually training and evaluating the system (Section~\ref{sec:experiments}) that we report explicitly because they generalize beyond this one paper: a naive implementation of weight-conditioned multi-objective RL can silently fail to condition on the weight at all; naively scalarizing raw per-step reward deltas with a Tchebycheff ideal point is a distinct, easy-to-miss bug from scalarizing the objectives themselves; a single-fixed-start greedy evaluation protocol that is valid on one city's action-space geometry can silently fail on another's; non-deterministic GPU scatter-reduction in a standard GNN message-passing implementation can be severe enough to invert which of two architectures a head-to-head comparison appears to favor, discovered only by re-running an identical configuration more than once rather than trusting a single result; and a checkpoint-saving routine correctly scoped for cross-city transfer (excluding a city-specific input layer) can silently produce an incompletely-restored model when reused, unmodified, for same-city post-hoc evaluation.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Related Work}\label{sec:related}

\subsection{The Transport Network Design Problem}

MNEP inherits its difficulty from the broader Transport Network Design Problem (TNDP), a decades-old family of network-design formulations spanning road, bus, and rail network planning under budget constraints \citep{farahani2013}. \citeauthor{farahani2013}'s review catalogues the dominant solution families -- mixed-integer and bilevel programming, genetic algorithms, simulated annealing, tabu search, ant colony optimization -- and a recurring pattern across nearly all of them: tractability is bought by constraining the search space before optimization begins, typically by restricting candidate infrastructure to a small number of hand-drawn corridors or by fixing the number of lines in advance. This is a reasonable engineering compromise for classical combinatorial optimization, where the size of the true search space (every possible subset and sequencing of candidate stations, evaluated against every possible resulting demand redistribution) is well beyond what exact or even metaheuristic methods can explore directly. It is also precisely the compromise that framing TNDP as a sequential decision process, solvable by reinforcement learning, is designed to remove: an RL agent explores the \emph{full} unconstrained space of station-by-station construction choices, learning through repeated simulated rollouts which sequences tend to produce networks that serve the city well, without a human ever having to pre-select the plausible corridors.

This particular move -- replacing hand-constrained search with a learned, sequential-construction policy -- is not unique to transportation, and it is worth situating MNEP within the broader pattern before returning to the transportation-specific literature. \citeauthor{bengio2021}'s methodological survey of machine learning for combinatorial optimization \citep{bengio2021} identifies exactly this pattern across vehicle routing, scheduling, and network design more broadly: problems with an intractably large solution space, historically solved by hand-crafted heuristics or metaheuristics whose quality depends heavily on domain-specific engineering, are increasingly reformulated as sequential decision processes where a learned policy constructs a solution step by step, using function approximation (often, as here, a GNN) to generalize across problem instances that hand-crafted heuristics would need to be re-tuned for individually. MNEP is, in this framing, a specific instance of a general and rapidly growing methodological pattern, which is one more reason we think it matters that a method combining that pattern's two most consequential recent refinements -- graph-structured representation learning and multi-objective decomposition -- has not, prior to this paper, been applied jointly to it.

\subsection{Reinforcement learning for MNEP}

Three papers have applied this idea specifically to metro network expansion, and each makes a different methodological bet.

\citet{su2024} (MetroGNN) bet on spatial representation learning. They represent candidate urban regions as a heterogeneous multi-graph -- one edge type for spatial contiguity, one for OD-flow association -- and train an attentive actor-critic policy over this graph, with an action mask enforcing network-design feasibility constraints (station spacing, turn angles, remaining budget) so every rollout produces a physically buildable line. Graph neural networks are, by now, a mature enough tool that their applicability here is unsurprising in retrospect \citep{wu2021gnn}: a candidate region's usefulness as a next station depends on its neighbors' properties as much as its own, which is exactly the kind of relational structure GNNs are built to exploit. The payoff is substantial -- MetroGNN improves OD demand satisfaction over prior heuristic and RL baselines by 15.9\% on average across their evaluated scenarios, exceeding 30\% in higher-budget, more complex cases -- but the method optimizes a single objective (demand satisfaction) and trains a fresh policy from scratch for every city it is applied to.

\citet{zhang2024} bet on fairness instead of representational sophistication. Demand-maximizing policies, theirs included when run in single-objective mode, have a well-known failure mode in transportation planning: because travel demand correlates strongly with existing wealth and connectivity, a policy that purely maximizes satisfied OD flow tends to route new infrastructure toward areas that are already comparatively well served, reproducing and amplifying the very access disparities that public transit investment is nominally meant to correct \citep{hansen1959,raza2023}. \citeauthor{zhang2024} address this by formulating the reward as a three-dimensional vector -- OD demand, a social-equity term, and a gravity-style accessibility measure built on the radiation model of human mobility \citep{simini2012}, which predicts trip flows between locations from population density alone, without requiring a fitted gravity-law exponent -- and combining the three via Tchebycheff decomposition \citep{zhangli2007}, a scalarization technique from the multi-objective optimization literature \citep{hayes2022} whose key property, relative to a simple weighted sum, is that it can reach solutions in non-convex regions of the Pareto front that a weighted sum provably cannot. Sampling a fresh weight vector each training episode, rather than fixing one in advance, lets a single trained policy explore the full range of demand/equity/accessibility trade-offs instead of committing to one planner's a priori judgment about their relative importance. The resulting method outperforms traditional heuristic algorithms (genetic algorithm, ant colony optimization, mathematical programming) by over 30\% and their own weighted-sum RL variant by a smaller margin, evaluated on a real-world Xi'an case study -- but it represents state with hand-engineered features rather than a graph-structured encoder, so it cannot exploit spatial relational structure the way MetroGNN does.

\citet{michailidis2026} bet against both of the preceding papers' machinery. They reformulate MNEP as a Non-Markovian Reward Decision Process, a reformulation that shrinks the effective state-action space enough that a tabular Monte Carlo RL method -- a plain lookup-table policy, no neural network, no GPU -- with fairness-based reward functions matches deep RL performance while using 18$\times$ fewer training episodes and producing an estimated 12$\times$ less CO$_2$ over the training run. This is a pointed and well-evidenced critique of the field's default assumption that more representational capacity is always worth its computational cost, and it is evaluated carefully -- but only on Xi'an and Amsterdam, both mid-sized, data-rich, already-mature metro systems whose state-action spaces, however large in absolute terms, may not be representative of either extreme this paper is concerned with: the enormous scale of the world's largest metro networks, or the acute data sparsity of metro systems still in their first decade of operation.

Read together, these three papers span the design space's most important axes -- spatial representation, multi-objective fairness, computational efficiency -- without any single paper occupying more than one axis at a time, and none reports a result outside a small set of already-mature systems. \subsection{Related operational-phase work on rail-bus coordination}

\citet{kang2019} study a related but structurally different problem: coordinating last-train timetables with bus bridging services on an \emph{existing} urban railway network, via a decomposable mixed-integer linear program, showing that careful coordination of last-train connections across lines together with dispatched bridging buses substantially reduces the number of stranded transfer passengers at service end-of-day. This is an operational-phase problem -- it assumes a fixed rail topology and optimizes how the network is \emph{run}, not what the network \emph{is} -- so we treat it as related work rather than a methodological component of our approach; we neither model bus bridging nor optimize timetables. What it does establish, and what we lean on directly in Section~\ref{sec1}, is that bus and rail service are planned and evaluated jointly in operational practice, not in isolation. That observation is the whole justification for treating a city's existing bus network as a legitimate input signal to rail network \emph{design} (Section~\ref{sec:data}) rather than dismissing it as out of scope for a rail-focused study; \citeauthor{kang2019}'s result is evidence for the premise, not a method we build on.

\section{Problem Formulation and Method}\label{sec:formulation-method}

\subsection{Problem Formulation}\label{sec:problem}

It is worth being precise about what, exactly, an agent is doing when it ``builds a metro line'' inside this formulation, because the abstraction hides a design decision that matters. The agent does not place stations on a blank map; it walks a graph that already encodes everything we know about the city -- where people live, where they travel to, which regions border which, how well each region is currently served by rail and by bus -- and at each step it commits to exactly one irreversible extension of the network under construction, the same way a real transit authority commits budget to one segment before moving to the next. Framing the problem this way, as a finite-horizon Markov Decision Process (MDP) over a graph of candidate regions, follows \citet{su2024} and \citet{zhang2024} and buys two things classical TNDP solvers do not get for free: the agent can be evaluated and improved through simulated rollouts rather than exhaustive search, and the same formulation, with a different reward vector, is what lets us later condition a single trained policy on a whole family of demand/equity/accessibility trade-offs rather than solving the optimization problem once per trade-off.

Formally, let $\mathcal{N} = \{n_1, \dots, n_k\}$ be the set of candidate regions (Section~\ref{sec:data}). A rail network at step $t$ is a graph $M_t = (V_t, E_t)$ with $V_t \subseteq \mathcal{N}$ the selected stations and $E_t$ the line segments connecting them.

\textbf{State.} $S_t = (M_t, b_t)$: the partial rail network built so far, together with the remaining construction budget. This is deliberately a thin state -- it does not separately track, say, which OD pairs have already been satisfied, because that information is fully recoverable from $M_t$ and the (fixed, precomputed) OD matrix, and keeping the state minimal keeps the GNN encoder's job well-defined: encode the network as it currently stands, not a growing bundle of derived statistics. Bus-derived features (candidate-region richness, demand-proxy signal; Section~\ref{sec:data}) are encoded as node/edge attributes within $M_t$'s graph representation, not as a separate part of the state that could itself be modified by an action -- this is the formal expression of the scope boundary argued for in Section~\ref{sec1}: bus data can inform what the agent sees, never what it is allowed to do.

\textbf{Action.} $A_t \in \mathcal{N} \setminus V_t$: the agent selects one unselected candidate node to add, either extending an existing line or starting a new one. Not every unselected node is a legal choice at every step -- a station that would violate minimum spacing from its neighbors, or force an implausibly sharp turn in the line, is not a station a real transit authority would build -- so the action space is restricted to a feasibility mask $\mathcal{A}(S_t) \subseteq \mathcal{N} \setminus V_t$ enforcing station-spacing and turn-angle constraints and remaining budget, as in \citet{su2024}. $\mathcal{A}(S_t)$ never contains a bus-route action; only rail (metro/tram/light-rail) extensions are ever selectable, per the scope decision in Section~\ref{sec1}.

\textbf{Transition.} Deterministic: $M_{t+1} = M_t \cup \{A_t\}$, with the new station connected to the nearest node on the same line (or starting a new line if none exists within a feasibility-preserving distance). $b_{t+1} = b_t - \text{cost}(A_t)$. An episode terminates when $b_t$ is exhausted or $\mathcal{A}(S_t) = \emptyset$ -- the second condition matters in practice for small, sparse candidate graphs (Hanoi, Ho Chi Minh City) where the agent can paint itself into a corner well before the budget runs out.

\textbf{Reward.} Three network-level objectives are tracked as bounded, cumulative functions of the network built so far: $C^{\text{demand}}(M_t) \in [0,1]$ follows \citeauthor{su2024}'s OD-demand-satisfaction formulation, weighting each OD pair's flow by how directly $M_t$ connects its endpoints; $C^{\text{equity}}(M_t) \in [0,1]$ is one minus a Gini index over per-region (or, in the Xi'an implementation of Section~\ref{sec:experiments}, per price-based population group) demand satisfaction, so that a perfectly equal distribution of access scores 1 and a maximally unequal one scores 0; and $C^{\text{access}}(M_t) \in [0,1]$ is an accessibility measure over the same per-region granularity. Concretely, for a vector of per-group cumulative satisfied demand $\mathbf{x} = (x_1, \dots, x_m)$, sorted ascending,
\begin{equation}
\text{Gini}(\mathbf{x}) = \frac{m + 1 - 2 \sum_{i=1}^{m} \frac{(m - i + 1) x_i}{\sum_j x_j}}{m}, \qquad C^{\text{equity}}(M_t) = 1 - \text{Gini}(\mathbf{x}), \label{eq:gini}
\end{equation}
the standard Lorenz-curve-based inequality index used elsewhere in the transit-equity literature \citep{raza2023}. We flag a construct-validity concern with the price-based grouping at the point it is defined, rather than only in the Discussion: housing price is not an exogenous label but is itself partly a function of existing transit accessibility (well-documented capitalization effects in the transit-equity literature), so a household already in a high-price band may be there partly \emph{because} it already enjoys good access -- grouping by price band and then measuring whether \emph{new} access is equally distributed across price bands risks a degree of circularity that a need-based grouping (car ownership, income net of housing capitalization, age) would not share. We do not have the data to construct such a grouping for Xi'an's benchmark environment (Section~\ref{sec:data}) and so report the price-band result as what it is -- a common, tractable proxy used elsewhere in this literature, not a fully construct-valid equity measure -- rather than either withholding it or presenting it with more confidence than this limitation warrants; Ho Chi Minh City's income-bracket grouping (Appendix~\ref{secA1}) is a closer approximation but shares the same underlying concern to a lesser degree. The design-intent accessibility measure is the radiation model of \citet{simini2012}, as used by \citet{zhang2024}, which predicts the trip flow available between region pairs from population density alone, without a fitted gravity-law exponent; the Xi'an experiment in Section~\ref{sec:experiments} instead uses a simpler, explicitly-labeled coverage proxy,
\begin{equation}
C^{\text{coverage}}(M_t) = \frac{1}{|\mathcal{N}|} \sum_{n_i \in \mathcal{N}} \mathbb{1}\left[\min_{n_j \in V_t} d(n_i, n_j) \le K\right], \label{eq:coverage}
\end{equation}
the fraction of all candidate regions within Chebyshev distance $K$ of some already-built station -- related in spirit to the radiation model (both reward geographically-distributed rather than clustered station placement) but not numerically equivalent to it, a distinction Section~\ref{sec:discussion} returns to as an explicit limitation rather than an implementation detail elided in a footnote. All objectives are computed from the same underlying GNN-encoded region graph (Section~\ref{sec:method}) so they can share intermediate representations rather than requiring three separate forward passes.

\textbf{Scalarization and objective.} Following \citet{zhang2024}, at the start of each training episode a weight vector $\mathbf{w}$ is sampled uniformly from the probability simplex (Algorithm~\ref{alg:curriculum}, line 5), and the three cumulative objectives are combined via augmented Tchebycheff decomposition \citep{zhangli2007},
\begin{equation}
g(\mathbf{C}(M_t) \mid \mathbf{w}, \mathbf{z}^*) = \max_j \left[ w_j \left| C^{(j)}(M_t) - z^*_j \right| \right] + \varepsilon \sum_j w_j \left| C^{(j)}(M_t) - z^*_j \right|,\label{eq:tchebycheff}
\end{equation}
with ideal point $\mathbf{z}^* = (1,1,1)$ and small $\varepsilon > 0$ to exclude weakly-Pareto-optimal solutions, matching the implementation in \texttt{src/rl/reward.py}. Crucially, Tchebycheff's ideal-point comparison is only meaningful against the bounded cumulative objectives $\mathbf{C}(M_t)$ themselves, \emph{not} against their raw per-step deltas -- a per-step demand gain is typically a small fraction of one percent, and comparing it directly to an ideal point of 1 would make every step look equally, uninformatively far from ideal regardless of which action was actually taken. The reward the policy is trained on is therefore the step-to-step change in the already-scalarized, sign-flipped value,
\begin{equation}
r_t = -g(\mathbf{C}(M_{t+1}) \mid \mathbf{w}, \mathbf{z}^*) + g(\mathbf{C}(M_t) \mid \mathbf{w}, \mathbf{z}^*), \label{eq:marginal-reward}
\end{equation}
which telescopes over an episode to the total improvement in scalarized objective value, exactly analogous in spirit to the raw-objective marginal reward $C^{(j)}(M_{t+1}) - C^{(j)}(M_t)$ used by \citet{su2024} for their single demand objective, but applied after scalarization rather than before it. Section~\ref{sec:experiments} reports this distinction not as a footnote but as a real methodological finding: an earlier version of our implementation applied Tchebycheff scalarization to the raw per-objective deltas directly, which trains but silently fails to make the policy responsive to $\mathbf{w}$ at all, since the scalarization term is dominated by the (constant, near-1) distance from a near-zero delta to the ideal point regardless of which objective actually improved.

The policy $\pi_\theta$ is trained to maximize the expected episodic sum of these scalarized rewards, $\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{w}}\left[\sum_t r_t\right]$, over the sampled weight distribution (undiscounted: each finite-horizon episode ends when the construction budget is exhausted, per the Transition paragraph above). For this expectation to actually depend on $\mathbf{w}$ in a way the policy can exploit, $\mathbf{w}$ must also be visible to the policy itself, not only to the reward function that trains it -- Section~\ref{sec:method-policy} makes this concrete. Done correctly, a single trained policy supports the full range of demand/equity/accessibility trade-offs rather than committing to one fixed weighting at training time; done incorrectly, as our own first implementation attempt was, the weight vector influences the training signal but the resulting policy never learns to look at it, and behaves identically regardless of which trade-off is requested at deployment time. We return to exactly how we detected and fixed this in Section~\ref{sec:experiments}, because we think the failure mode is general enough to be worth naming for other implementers of weight-conditioned multi-objective RL, not specific to our codebase.

\subsubsection{A worked example}\label{sec:problem-example}

The scalarize-then-difference distinction in Eq.~\ref{eq:marginal-reward} is easy to state abstractly and easy to get backwards in practice, so it is worth working through with small, illustrative (not real-data) numbers. Suppose at step $t$ the network has $C^{\text{demand}}(M_t) = 0.10$, $C^{\text{equity}}(M_t) = 0.60$, $C^{\text{access}}(M_t) = 0.40$, and the agent's next action improves these to $C^{\text{demand}}(M_{t+1}) = 0.11$, $C^{\text{equity}}(M_{t+1}) = 0.62$, $C^{\text{access}}(M_{t+1}) = 0.41$ -- small, realistic per-step gains, since one station rarely transforms a city-scale objective in a single step. Under equal weights $\mathbf{w} = (1/3, 1/3, 1/3)$ and ideal point $\mathbf{z}^* = (1,1,1)$, the \emph{incorrect} approach -- scalarizing the raw deltas $(0.01, 0.02, 0.01)$ directly -- computes $\max_j[w_j |{\Delta_j - 1}|] \approx \max_j[\frac{1}{3}(0.99), \frac{1}{3}(0.98), \frac{1}{3}(0.99)] \approx 0.33$ regardless of which action was taken, because every plausible single-step delta is close to zero and therefore close to maximally far from an ideal point of 1; two actions with very different quality profiles -- one that improves equity substantially at demand's expense, one that does the opposite -- would receive nearly identical scalarized rewards under this scheme, destroying the training signal's ability to discriminate between them. The \emph{correct} approach scalarizes the cumulative values themselves at each timestep, $g(\mathbf{C}(M_t)) \approx \max_j[\frac{1}{3}(0.90), \frac{1}{3}(0.40), \frac{1}{3}(0.60)] = 0.30$ and $g(\mathbf{C}(M_{t+1})) \approx \max_j[\frac{1}{3}(0.89), \frac{1}{3}(0.38), \frac{1}{3}(0.59)] \approx 0.297$, and the reward is their (sign-flipped) difference, $r_t \approx 0.30 - 0.297 = 0.003$ -- small, as a genuinely small single-step improvement should be, but non-degenerate, and sensitive to exactly which objective the max term is currently binding on (here, demand, the furthest from its ideal value, so an action that specifically improves demand moves the max term, and therefore $r_t$, more than an equally-sized improvement to equity or accessibility would). This is the behavior Eq.~\ref{eq:marginal-reward} is meant to produce, and Section~\ref{sec:experiments} reports what happened when our own early implementation instead did the former.

\subsection{Method}\label{sec:method}

The method has three pieces, each answering a different one of the gaps identified in Section~\ref{sec1}. A graph neural network encoder (Section~\ref{sec:method-gnn}) gives the policy access to the same spatially-structured city representation that makes MetroGNN effective, extended to also see bus-informed auxiliary signal where a city's rail network alone is too sparse. An attentive, weight-conditioned actor-critic policy (Section~\ref{sec:method-policy}) turns that representation into station-by-station construction decisions, and is explicitly built so that the Tchebycheff weight vector sampled each episode (Section~\ref{sec:problem}) is visible to the policy itself, not only to the reward that trains it -- the distinction that Section~\ref{sec:problem} flagged as a real, easy-to-miss implementation pitfall. A cross-city curriculum with prioritized replay and reward shaping (Section~\ref{sec:method-curriculum}) is the direct response to \citeauthor{michailidis2026}'s efficiency critique: rather than accept that GNN-based MNEP methods must be expensive to train, we ask whether training cost can be amortized across cities the way it already is, informally, across the training episodes within one city.

\subsubsection{GNN state encoder}\label{sec:method-gnn}

We encode $M_t$ with a two-relation heterogeneous graph neural network, following \citeauthor{su2024}'s scheme (\citeyear{su2024}, Sec.~3.1--3.2), implemented in \texttt{src/gnn/encoder.py}. Each candidate region $n_i$ has a raw feature vector $\mathbf{a}_i$ (population density, income/employment where available, bus-derived candidate-region and demand-proxy signal from Section~\ref{sec:data}, and existing-rail proximity), projected to an initial hidden state $\mathbf{h}_i^{(0)} = \mathbf{W}_A \mathbf{a}_i$. Two edge types connect candidate regions: spatial-contiguity edges $e^s_{ij}$ (shared boundary, Section~\ref{sec:data}'s queen-contiguity construction) and OD-flow edges $e^o_{ij}$ (disaggregated travel demand). At each layer $l$, messages are aggregated separately per relation and combined with a residual connection:
\begin{align}
\mathbf{h}_{s,i}^{(l)} &= \sum_{j} e^s_{ij} \, \mathbf{W}_s^{(l)} \mathbf{h}_j^{(l)}, \qquad
\mathbf{h}_{o,i}^{(l)} = \sum_{j} e^o_{ij} \, \mathbf{W}_o^{(l)} \mathbf{h}_j^{(l)}, \label{eq:gnn-relations}\\
\mathbf{h}_i^{(l+1)} &= \tanh\!\left( \mathbf{W}_c^{(l)} \left[\mathbf{h}_{s,i}^{(l)} \,\|\, \mathbf{h}_{o,i}^{(l)}\right] + \mathbf{h}_i^{(l)} \right), \label{eq:gnn-combine}
\end{align}
where $\|$ denotes concatenation. Stacking $L$ such layers yields final node embeddings $\mathbf{h}_i^{(L)}$ that jointly encode a region's rail/OD structure and its bus-informed auxiliary features -- the bus signal enters only as an entry of $\mathbf{a}_i$, never as its own node or edge type, so it cannot be selected as an action regardless of how the graph is encoded.

What this buys, concretely, over the flat feature vector used by \citet{zhang2024} (and by our own \texttt{flat\_encoder} ablation, Section~\ref{sec:experiments}), is that a region's usefulness as a candidate extension is allowed to depend on its neighbors, not only on its own raw attributes. A region with modest population density but bordering three high-demand neighbors along spatial-contiguity edges, or sitting on a high-volume OD-flow edge to a district the network does not yet reach, should plausibly score higher than an equally-populated region in a low-demand, poorly-connected part of the city -- information a flat feature vector simply does not have access to, since it has no notion of \emph{which} other regions are relevant to a given one, only what each region's own attributes are. Each additional message-passing layer $l$ extends how far this relational information propagates: after one layer, $\mathbf{h}_i^{(1)}$ reflects $n_i$'s immediate neighbors; after $L=2$ layers (Appendix~\ref{secA1}), it reflects neighbors of neighbors, letting the encoder capture second-order structure -- a region two hops from a major transit hub, for instance -- without requiring that structure to be hand-engineered into $\mathbf{a}_i$ as an explicit feature the way a non-graph method would need to.

\subsubsection{Multi-objective policy}\label{sec:method-policy}

The actor-critic policy (\texttt{src/rl/policy.py}) operates on the embedded graph $\{\mathbf{h}_i^{(L)}\}$. A query vector $\mathbf{q}_t$ represents the current partial network, computed by mean-pooling the embeddings of already-selected nodes $V_t$ (or all candidate nodes if $V_t = \emptyset$, at the start of an episode). Each candidate node is scored by scaled dot-product attention against $\mathbf{q}_t$,
\begin{equation}
\text{score}(n_i) = \frac{(\mathbf{W}_k \mathbf{h}_i^{(L)}) \cdot (\mathbf{W}_q \mathbf{q}_t)}{\sqrt{d}}, \label{eq:policy-score}
\end{equation}
with $d$ the embedding dimension, and infeasible actions ($n_i \notin \mathcal{A}(S_t)$) masked to $-\infty$ before a softmax yields the action distribution $\pi_\theta(A_t \mid S_t)$. A separate value head, a two-layer MLP over $\mathbf{q}_t$, estimates $V_\phi(S_t)$ for the actor-critic baseline -- the general actor-critic template we follow, learning a policy and a value function jointly rather than either alone, goes back to \citet{konda2000}, and remains the standard choice whenever the action space is large enough that pure value-based methods (learning a value for every state-action pair directly) become impractical, as it is here.

Conditioning the policy on the sampled Tchebycheff weight vector $\mathbf{w}$ is what makes the multi-objective claim in Section~\ref{sec:problem} actually true rather than aspirational, and it is worth stating precisely because it is easy to get subtly wrong: it is not enough for $\mathbf{w}$ to shape the \emph{reward} the policy is trained on, because a policy trained on a randomly varying reward signal, with no way to observe which variant it is currently being asked to optimize, can at best learn some compromise behavior averaged over the sampled weight distribution -- it has no mechanism to specialize its behavior to any particular $\mathbf{w}$, since $\mathbf{w}$ never enters its input. We embed $\mathbf{w}$ through a small learned linear layer and add it directly to the pooled query, $\mathbf{q}_t' = \mathbf{q}_t + \mathbf{W}_e \mathbf{w}$, before it is used both for attention scoring (Eq.~\ref{eq:policy-score}, with $\mathbf{q}_t'$ in place of $\mathbf{q}_t$) and for the value estimate. This is a small architectural change -- one additional linear layer -- but Section~\ref{sec:experiments} shows it is the difference between a policy that behaves identically under demand-only, equity-only, and coverage-only weightings and one that genuinely does not.

\subsubsection{Training procedure}\label{sec:method-training}

We train with Monte Carlo REINFORCE and a learned baseline, following the general actor-critic template of \citet{konda2000}, rather than a temporal-difference method: because each episode is a short, finite-horizon construction sequence ($nr\_stations$ steps, Appendix~\ref{secA1}) rather than an ongoing control task, the full-episode Monte Carlo return is cheap to compute exactly and avoids the bias temporal-difference bootstrapping would introduce for a horizon this short. For an episode with scalarized per-step rewards $r_0, \dots, r_{T-1}$ (Eq.~\ref{eq:marginal-reward}), the return-to-go at step $t$ is the undiscounted sum $G_t = \sum_{t'=t}^{T-1} r_{t'}$ (Section~\ref{sec:problem} motivates the choice of $\gamma = 1$), and the advantage used for the policy gradient is $\hat{A}_t = G_t - V_\phi(S_t)$, normalized within each episode ($\hat{A}_t \leftarrow (\hat{A}_t - \bar{A}) / (\sigma_A + 10^{-6})$ when $\sigma_A$ is non-negligible) to stabilize the gradient scale across episodes whose returns can vary substantially in magnitude depending on the sampled Tchebycheff weight vector. The combined loss is
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{L}(\theta, \phi) = -\frac{1}{T} \sum_{t=0}^{T-1} \hat{A}_t \log \pi_\theta(A_t \mid S_t) + \frac{1}{2} \cdot \text{MSE}\!\left(V_\phi(S_t), G_t\right), \label{eq:loss}
\end{equation}
optimized jointly over the GNN encoder, policy, value-head, and weight-embedding parameters with Adam and gradient-norm clipping (Appendix~\ref{secA1}); the value-loss coefficient of $\tfrac{1}{2}$ and the clipping threshold are standard defaults in this literature, not tuned specifically for MNEP, since we wanted the reported results to reflect the method working with unremarkable hyperparameters rather than a configuration hand-fit to this one city.

\subsubsection{Cross-city curriculum and sample efficiency}\label{sec:method-curriculum}

\citeauthor{michailidis2026}'s central empirical claim -- that a tabular RL method, needing no neural network and no GPU, matches deep RL's performance at a fraction of the training cost and carbon footprint -- is not a claim we set out to refute. It is a claim we take as a design constraint: if the value of a GNN-based, multi-objective policy is going to justify its additional computational cost, that value has to come from somewhere real, not from ignoring an efficiency argument that the evidence otherwise supports. Two places suggest themselves. The first is scale: a lookup table's efficiency advantage on a small, mid-sized network like Xi'an's may not hold once the state-action space is as large as Chengdu's, where the sheer number of candidate regions could make tabular methods' implicit assumption -- that most state-action pairs will be visited often enough to estimate their value reliably -- start to break down, though establishing this is squarely future work (Section~\ref{sec:discussion}) rather than a claim this paper's two-city (Xi'an, Ho Chi Minh City) result can support. The second, and the one we act on directly here, is that a GNN encoder's training cost need not be paid independently by every city, the way both \citeauthor{michailidis2026}'s tabular method and every one of the three reference RL papers effectively require: a lookup table learned on one city's grid has no structure that transfers to a different city's grid, but a GNN's learned message-passing weights are, in principle, city-agnostic, since they operate on the same relative notions of spatial contiguity and OD-flow structure regardless of which city's graph they are applied to.

We therefore introduce a cross-city curriculum: policy weights are pretrained on the smaller networks (in our full four-city design, Hanoi and Xi'an) and used to initialize training on the larger networks (Ho Chi Minh City, Chengdu), rather than training each city's policy from scratch, following the general intuition -- well established in supervised deep learning, less commonly exploited across environments in RL -- that a network which has already learned useful low-level structure converges faster from that starting point than from a random initialization. This is combined with prioritized experience replay \citep{schaul2016}, which samples training transitions in proportion to the magnitude of their temporal-difference error rather than uniformly, so the limited training budget is spent disproportionately on the transitions the policy is currently getting most wrong, and potential-based reward shaping, which accelerates credit assignment for the delayed, sparse parts of the reward signal without altering the optimal policy the agent ultimately converges to. Algorithm~\ref{alg:curriculum} states the full procedure; Section~\ref{sec:experiments} reports results from the first city in this sequence, Xi'an, trained from a random initialization since no smaller city had yet been trained to transfer from, and Section~\ref{sec:experiments-curriculum} reports the measured test of the curriculum step itself, warm-starting Ho Chi Minh City from Xi'an's trained weights, under both a matched training budget (which speaks to transfer's effect on asymptotic quality) and a reduced budget one fifth its size (which speaks directly to the efficiency gains -- fewer episodes to reach a given quality -- the curriculum is actually motivated by).

\begin{algorithm}
\caption{Cross-city curriculum training for the GNN multi-objective policy}\label{alg:curriculum}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require Cities ordered by network scale $C_1 \le C_2 \le \dots \le C_n$; Tchebycheff weight set $W$
\State Initialize policy/value network parameters $\theta_0$ randomly
\For{$i = 1, \dots, n$}
    \State Initialize $\theta \gets \theta_{i-1}$ \Comment{transfer weights from the previous, smaller city}
    \For{each training episode on city $C_i$}
        \State Sample Tchebycheff weight vector $w \sim W$
        \State Roll out policy on $C_i$'s region graph, collect transitions $(s_t, a_t, r_t, s_{t+1})$
        \State Store transitions in prioritized replay buffer $\mathcal{D}_i$
        \State Sample prioritized batch from $\mathcal{D}_i$, apply potential-based reward shaping
        \State Update $\theta$ via actor-critic gradient step on the scalarized reward
    \EndFor
    \State $\theta_i \gets \theta$
\EndFor
\State \Return $\theta_n$
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\subsubsection{Instantiating the action space per city}\label{sec:method-implementation}

Section~\ref{sec:problem} defines the action space abstractly, as selecting any feasible unselected candidate node $A_t \in \mathcal{A}(S_t)$. In practice, how that feasibility mask is realized depends on how a given city's candidate regions are represented, and it is worth being explicit that this varies across our case-study cities rather than presenting a single uniform implementation as if it were forced by the formulation. For Xi'an (Section~\ref{sec:experiments}), we adopt the standard benchmark environment for this exact city and dataset -- an 8-directional grid walk, where the agent occupies one grid cell and may extend the line to any of its eight compass neighbors subject to the feasibility mask -- because this is the action-space convention used by \citet{michailidis2026}'s own public environment, and matching it exactly is what makes a direct, apples-to-apples comparison against their reported numbers possible rather than merely approximate. For Ho Chi Minh City's commune/ward-level candidate regions (Section~\ref{sec:data}), where regions are irregular polygons rather than uniform grid cells, the natural realization is instead \citeauthor{su2024}'s formulation: the action space is any spatially-contiguous unselected neighbor of the current line's endpoint, which for an irregular polygon graph is exactly analogous to what an 8-directional grid walk is for a regular one. Both are legal specializations of the same abstract MDP in Section~\ref{sec:problem}; neither changes the reward, the objectives, or the GNN encoder's architecture. We flag this explicitly because it is the kind of implementation detail that is easy to leave implicit in a way that makes results across cities look more directly comparable than they actually are -- Section~\ref{sec:discussion} returns to what this does and does not license us to conclude when comparing across cities with different action-space realizations. \section{Case Studies and Data}\label{sec:data}

The four cities anchoring this evaluation were not chosen for convenience; each one is doing a specific piece of argumentative work that the reference literature's city choices could not. Xi'an, a provincial capital of roughly thirteen million people in Shaanxi, China, has already been studied by two of our three reference papers -- \citet{michailidis2026} used it to argue tabular RL suffices, and \citet{zhang2024} used it to demonstrate the value of multi-objective fairness -- which makes it, unusually for this literature, a city where our own results can be checked against two independently reported baselines on the same underlying network rather than only our own reimplementations of them. Chengdu, roughly the same population but with one of the most extensive metro networks anywhere in the world after a decade of construction that shows no sign of slowing, is the scale extreme neither prior paper tested: if a tabular method's efficiency advantage is going to break down anywhere, or if a GNN's spatial reasoning is going to earn its computational cost anywhere, Chengdu's state-action space is large enough that the answer should become visible. Chengdu's metro network is also among the most extensively studied by Chinese transportation researchers, a network whose growth and operating data other groups know intimately -- a reason to hold our Chengdu results to a higher standard of scrutiny, not a lower one. Hanoi and Ho Chi Minh City are the opposite extreme, and the one the reference literature has never touched at all: Vietnam's two largest metropolitan areas, home to a combined seventeen million people, are only now building the metro networks that Xi'an and Chengdu have had for a decade or more, which makes them exactly the kind of small, data-sparse, actively-expanding system this paper's motivating scope decision (Section~\ref{sec1}) was designed for -- and exactly the kind of system where getting network expansion planning right, before path-dependence sets in, matters most.

Because MNEP RL methods are trained per city rather than pooled across a fixed benchmark set, the city set is deliberately not fixed at four architecturally -- nothing in the method requires exactly this set, and we extend it with additional Asian cities (candidates: Beijing and Changsha, the two cities used by \citet{su2024}, if their released code/data prove usable) wherever open data can be confirmed, since a larger and more diverse city set strengthens both the transfer-learning claim of Section~\ref{sec:method-curriculum} and the scale-dependent generalization claim motivating the Chengdu comparison. \subsection{Data sources per city}

The sources below have been directly verified by inspecting the underlying repositories (not merely located), except where noted.

\textbf{Network topology and planned extensions.} Existing rail geometry for all four cities is obtainable from OpenStreetMap/Overpass. For Xi'an and Chengdu specifically, a public dataset covering 38 Chinese cities provides \emph{year-by-year} (2010--2020) station-point and line shapefiles, confirmed by direct inspection; because it is multi-year, it additionally enables a validation protocol not used by any of the three reference RL papers, worth spelling out concretely rather than only gesturing at: train the policy on a city's network as it existed in an earlier year (say, 2015), let it predict the highest-priority next extensions under the trained weight vectors, and check how closely those predictions match what the city's transit authority actually built by a later year (say, 2020). Chengdu is the more informative city to run this on precisely because it is data-rich enough that this check is possible at all -- a decade of near-continuous construction gives multiple candidate year-pairs to test, unlike Hanoi or Ho Chi Minh City, whose networks are too young to have an earlier ``before'' state worth comparing against. This protocol would not establish that the policy's recommendations are correct in any normative sense (a real transit authority's actual build sequence reflects budget, political, and engineering constraints well beyond travel demand alone, so disagreement with history is not necessarily evidence of a worse policy), but agreement would be a meaningfully stronger validation signal than any of the metrics in Table~\ref{tab:results} can provide alone, since it checks the method's recommendations against ground truth decisions made by people with real information the model does not have. Planned-extension maps exist as official planning diagrams for all four cities (Hanoi's 8-line master plan; Ho Chi Minh City's MAUR master plan; Xi'an's and Chengdu's Five-Year-Plan transport documents) but require manual digitization -- none is available as a ready-to-use shapefile.

\textbf{OD demand.} Ho Chi Minh City has by far the strongest confirmed source: the public \texttt{CityScope/CSL\_HCMC} repository (MIT Media Lab) ships real shapefiles -- metro routes/stations, bus routes/stops, a 2018 household-travel-survey OD matrix, district- and ward-level population, points of interest (POI), and ward-level census with age breakdown -- verified by direct inspection, though the repository carries no explicit license, so we treat it as research/fair-use only and do not redistribute the underlying files. For Xi'an, a public MIT-licensed environment (via \citet{michailidis2026}'s code release and its \texttt{mo-tndp} submodule) ships a ready-to-use 29$\times$29-cell grid with 463{,}010 nonzero OD pairs, the existing metro lines as grid-coordinate paths, and per-cell house-price/equity groupings -- confirmed by direct inspection. That dataset's own citation file attributes it to \citet{michailidis2024} rather than to the KDD~2020 paper we had provisionally assumed as its origin; we cite the confirmed source and do not assert the earlier, unverified lineage. Notably, \citet{zhang2024} -- one of our three reference RL baselines -- also uses Xi'an as its case study, so our Xi'an results can be compared against two, not just one, of the three reference methods' originally-reported numbers. For Hanoi and Chengdu (and as a fallback for the others), we construct a gravity-model demand proxy from WorldPop population-density rasters and OpenStreetMap/Amap/Baidu POI density, calibrated against published ridership figures (e.g., Hanoi's Cat Linh--Ha Dong line: 13.68M riders in 2025, up 15.3\% year-on-year; Ho Chi Minh City Line 1: 20.56M riders in 2025).

\textbf{OD disaggregation methodology.} Ho Chi Minh City's 2018 household travel survey reports OD flow between districts, coarser than the 322 commune/ward-level candidate regions the GNN encoder operates on (Section~\ref{sec:method-gnn}), so each surveyed district-level flow must be disaggregated down to the region level before it can populate an OD-flow edge $e^o_{ij}$. We do this by population share: if district $d$ contains regions $\{n_1, \dots, n_p\}$ with 2019 populations $\{\rho_1, \dots, \rho_p\}$, a surveyed flow $F_{d,d'}$ between districts $d$ and $d'$ is distributed to region pairs as
\begin{equation}
\hat{F}_{n_i, n_j} = F_{d,d'} \cdot \frac{\rho_i}{\sum_{n_k \in d} \rho_k} \cdot \frac{\rho_j}{\sum_{n_l \in d'} \rho_l}, \qquad n_i \in d,\ n_j \in d', \label{eq:od-disaggregation}
\end{equation}
a standard proportional-allocation approach when demand data is reported at a coarser spatial granularity than the model requires. Implementing this against the real survey data surfaced a real data-quality issue worth reporting as a methodological note in its own right: the survey's district-name field and the population shapefile's district-name field disagree on spacing for at least one district (``BinhTan'' in one source, ``Binh Tan'' in the other), which under naive exact-string matching caused 5 of 83 surveyed district-pairs -- representing a non-trivial share of total surveyed flow -- to be silently dropped rather than disaggregated, with no error or warning of any kind. We fixed this with a normalized-name fallback match (case-folded, whitespace- and punctuation-stripped) and, more importantly, added explicit logging of any district name that still fails to match after normalization, so that any future, still-unanticipated naming mismatch fails loudly rather than silently shrinking the effective dataset. We flag this not because the specific bug is significant on its own, but because ``does a join across two real-world government or survey datasets actually match every record it should'' is exactly the kind of question that is easy to assume rather than verify, and silent partial data loss of this kind would not have been visible in any downstream metric -- the pipeline would have produced a plausible-looking, quietly-wrong OD graph with no indication anything was missing.

\textbf{Bus network (auxiliary signal only).} Each city's existing bus route network is used solely as an auxiliary input to the rail expansion problem: it helps define a richer set of candidate expansion regions in cities where the rail network alone is too sparse (Hanoi, Ho Chi Minh City) and contributes an additional demand-proxy feature. Bus routes are never an output or design target of the method. Ho Chi Minh City's bus network is available as a real shapefile (CSL\_HCMC, above); the remaining cities use OpenStreetMap bus-route extraction. \emph{Coverage caveat, confirmed by direct inspection:} CSL\_HCMC's 189 bus stops fall entirely within a small downtown/pilot-study area, giving nonzero bus-derived signal for only $\sim$32 of the city's 322 candidate zones ($\sim$10\%); outside that area the bus feature is uniformly zero, and the OpenStreetMap fallback (or a wider bus dataset, if found) will be needed for full-city coverage.

\textbf{Administrative boundaries / candidate regions.} GADM (the Database of Global Administrative Areas) provides consistent district-level administrative boundaries for all four cities, usable to define MetroGNN-style candidate expansion regions.

\textbf{Coordinate systems.} Chinese POI sources (Amap, Baidu) report coordinates in the obfuscated GCJ-02 system, which must be converted to WGS84 before merging with OSM/metro station coordinates; this conversion step is required for Xi'an and Chengdu but not for the OSM-only Vietnamese cities.

\section{Experiments, Results, and Discussion}\label{sec:experiments}

We report results from the first two cities in the evaluation programme described in Section~\ref{sec:data}. Xi'an comes first because it is the one city with a ready-to-use, previously-published OD environment (Section~\ref{sec:data}), which let us validate the full pipeline -- data loading, GNN encoding, multi-objective policy training, and baseline comparison -- against a real, external, previously-studied dataset before extending it to cities where we ourselves had to assemble the data pipeline from raw sources. Ho Chi Minh City comes second, deliberately, because its irregular polygon graph and real household-survey OD data (Section~\ref{sec:data}) are structurally the closest analogue in this evaluation to the small, data-sparse emerging systems (Hanoi) that motivate this paper's scope in the first place, and because it is the first city with a trained predecessor (Xi'an) to test the cross-city curriculum (Section~\ref{sec:method-curriculum}) against. What follows is accordingly a two-city result, honestly scoped as such throughout: Chengdu, the paper's scale-extreme test, and Hanoi remain future work (Section~\ref{sec:discussion}). Section~\ref{sec:discussion} returns to exactly what these two results do and do not license us to conclude.

\subsection{Baselines}

We compare against four baselines. The tabular RL baseline is a direct, literal execution of \citeauthor{michailidis2026}'s own public code release (Section~\ref{sec:data}) on the same Xi'an data our method uses, not a reimplementation from the paper's description -- this is the strongest form of baseline comparison available, since any performance difference cannot be attributed to reimplementation drift. This revision adds a second non-RL baseline in response to a reviewer's observation that comparing exclusively against other RL methods leaves out the classical Transport Network Design Problem literature (Section~\ref{sec:related}) that remains transit planning's actual deployed alternative: a genetic algorithm, also run directly from \citeauthor{michailidis2026}'s own code release (\texttt{ga\_tndp.py}, which ships alongside the tabular Q-learning implementation but was not previously used in this paper), over the identical action space, feasibility mask, and station budget as every other method in this comparison. The other two reference methods, MetroGNN \citep{su2024} and the multi-objective actor-critic of \citet{zhang2024}, have no public code release we could locate and run directly, so we use the two natural ablations of our own method as principled stand-ins rather than either omitting the comparison or reimplementing from scratch under time pressure that would itself risk introducing errors: \texttt{single\_objective} (our full GNN architecture, trained with the Tchebycheff weight fixed to $(1,0,0)$ every episode, i.e.\ demand only) approximates MetroGNN's single-objective, GNN-based design; \texttt{flat\_encoder} (our full multi-objective reward and training procedure, but with the GNN encoder replaced by a linear projection with no message passing) approximates \citeauthor{zhang2024}'s multi-objective, non-graph-structured design. We are explicit that these are principled approximations of the two papers' \emph{design choices}, not reproductions of their \emph{reported numbers} -- our environment, feature set, and training budget all differ from theirs, so a numerical difference between our \texttt{single\_objective} row and \citeauthor{su2024}'s published results reflects those differences as much as anything about single-objective GNN policies in general. What the two ablations \emph{do} let us isolate cleanly, because everything except one architectural choice is held fixed, is the marginal contribution of the GNN representation and the marginal contribution of the multi-objective reward within our own method.

\subsection{Metrics}

We report four kinds of metric, each chosen to speak to a specific claim rather than as a generic checklist. OD demand satisfied (\%), read directly from the environment's own reward signal (Section~\ref{sec:method-implementation}), is the metric every one of the three reference papers reports and the one that makes a cross-paper comparison possible at all; social equity, computed as one minus the Gini index over the five price-based population groups' cumulative satisfied demand (Eq.~\ref{eq:gini}), is the metric that speaks directly to \citet{zhang2024}'s central concern and to this paper's own multi-objective claim; coverage, the fraction of grid cells within Chebyshev distance $K=2$ of any built station (Eq.~\ref{eq:coverage}), stands in for accessibility and speaks to the same claim from a geographic-spread angle. We flag explicitly, and repeat in Section~\ref{sec:discussion}'s limitations, that coverage is a simplified accessibility \emph{proxy} we introduce for this experiment, not \citeauthor{zhang2024}'s radiation-model accessibility measure \citep{simini2012} -- Xi'an's benchmark grid has no real population data attached to it, which makes the true radiation model uncomputable here without fabricating one, but reporting a proxy as though it were the exact published measure would misrepresent what was actually computed, so we do not. Section~\ref{sec:experiments-radiation} implements and evaluates the true radiation model for Ho Chi Minh City instead, where real population data makes it possible, and reports what that direct comparison against the coverage proxy actually shows. Training episodes and wall-clock time speak to \citeauthor{michailidis2026}'s efficiency claim directly, and we report real, measured CO$_2$ figures for the tabular baseline (Section~\ref{sec:experiments-carbon}) as a byproduct of running its own code with its own carbon-accounting instrumentation already attached; a directly comparable emissions figure for our own method awaits instrumenting our training script the same way, which we did not want to approximate from wall-clock time and a nominal GPU power draw rather than measure properly.

\subsection{Experimental setup}

All four rows of Table~\ref{tab:results} are trained under matched conditions: 500 training episodes, 20 stations per episode, on the real Xi'an 29$\times$29 candidate grid with existing metro lines included (Section~\ref{sec:data}). The tabular baseline uses its own paper's default hyperparameters; our method and both ablations share the hyperparameters in Appendix~\ref{secA1}. Full reproduction commands, including a real upstream bug we found and patched in the tabular baseline's own evaluation code, are recorded in \texttt{docs/baseline\_reproduction.md} in the project repository, described in detail below because we think the two bugs we found while getting this experiment running are informative beyond this one paper.

\textbf{Bug 1: a state-tracking error in the baseline's own test-time evaluation.} \citeauthor{michailidis2026}'s public \texttt{QLearningTNDP.test()} method took the agent's current grid position from the raw environment observation returned by \texttt{env.reset()}/\texttt{env.step()} (a \texttt{MultiBinary} vector encoding covered cells) rather than from \texttt{info['location\_grid\_index']} (a scalar grid index), as their own \texttt{train()} method -- two methods above it in the same file -- correctly does. A commented-out line in \texttt{test()} reveals that the intended fix was already known but never applied. The bug caused an \texttt{IndexError} on every attempted evaluation run; the training loop itself was unaffected and required no changes. We patched \texttt{test()} to match \texttt{train()}'s own convention. Because Table~\ref{tab:results}'s tabular-RL row is this paper's single most load-bearing comparative number, we verified the patch to a higher standard than trusting the patched method's own internal accounting: a separate, independently-written script loads each seed's saved Q-table directly and re-derives demand satisfaction from scratch (its own greedy rollout, its own accumulation of the environment's per-group reward vector), without importing or calling \texttt{QLearningTNDP.test()} at all. This independent re-derivation reproduced all five seeds' figures exactly (42: 1.836, 1: 1.552, 2: 1.888, 3: 0.783, 7: 0.750; mean 1.362, matching Table~\ref{tab:results}'s $1.36 \pm 0.56$), which we report as real confirmation rather than an assumed one. That same independent re-derivation surfaced a separate, smaller but worth-disclosing point about what ``Demand (\%)'' actually measures for both the tabular baseline and our own method (which computes it identically, Section~\ref{sec:method-training}): it is the \emph{sum} of five already-normalized per-group percentages (each a fraction of that price group's own total OD demand, since \texttt{od\_type=pct}), not a single demand-mass-weighted city-wide percentage. Re-deriving the latter directly from the environment's own per-group OD totals gives a smaller number for the same seed-42 rollout ($0.36\%$ demand-mass-weighted, versus $1.84\%$ as the sum-of-fractions figure Table~\ref{tab:results} reports) -- roughly a factor of five apart, the number of price groups, since summing five comparably-sized group fractions is not the same quantity as their demand-weighted average. Because both the tabular baseline and our own method compute ``Demand (\%)'' via this identical sum-of-group-fractions convention (inherited from the shared \texttt{mo-tndp} environment's native reward, not something either paper introduces separately), the \emph{relative} comparison between methods in Table~\ref{tab:results} is unaffected by this -- both sides are measured the same way -- but the absolute figures should be read as this specific, consistently-defined quantity rather than literally ``the percentage of all Xi'an OD demand satisfied,'' a distinction we had not previously stated explicitly and are glad this verification pass surfaced. We report this not to criticize a paper we otherwise rely on and whose central efficiency claim we take seriously, but because it is exactly the kind of small, easy-to-miss discrepancy between a paper's training code and its evaluation code that silently invalidates published benchmark numbers if it goes unnoticed, and because open, reusable research code -- which \citeauthor{michailidis2026} did the right thing by publishing -- is only as valuable as its verifiability.

\textbf{Bug 2: our own weight-conditioning gap.} Our first working version of the training loop reproduced the design in Section~\ref{sec:problem} on paper but not in code: the sampled Tchebycheff weight vector $\mathbf{w}$ shaped the training reward exactly as intended, but was never actually passed to the policy network, so the policy had no way to observe which trade-off it was being trained toward at any given moment. We caught this only because we built a direct sanity check into the training script: after training, evaluate the same trained model greedily under four different weight vectors (uniform, demand-only, equity-only, coverage-only) and check whether the resulting constructed lines actually differ. They did not -- byte-for-byte identical demand, equity, and coverage scores and an identical action sequence across all four weightings, which is conclusive evidence that the ``single policy, many trade-offs'' claim was false for that implementation, despite the training loss decreasing normally and the training-time metrics looking entirely unremarkable. Section~\ref{sec:method-policy}'s query-conditioning fix ($\mathbf{q}_t' = \mathbf{q}_t + \mathbf{W}_e\mathbf{w}$) resolves it; Table~\ref{tab:weight-sensitivity} below reports the same four-weighting sanity check on the corrected model, where the four settings now produce genuinely different lines. We think this failure mode -- a multi-objective RL method that trains without error, reports plausible-looking metrics, and is nonetheless not actually multi-objective in the sense claimed -- is worth naming explicitly for other implementers of weight-conditioned policies, since nothing about the failure is visible from the training curves alone; only an explicit behavioral check across weight settings reveals it. We would not have caught it, and would have reported a Table~\ref{tab:results} row for a method that silently was not doing what the paper claims, without deliberately building in this check.

Table~\ref{tab:results} reflects the complete picture across five seeds per method (matching the tabular baseline's own seed count, closing a limitation the original three-seed version of this table disclosed) rather than the single-seed snapshot we first drafted this section around, and we report the fuller picture in full, including where it complicates a cleaner story. Five findings stand out, all reported plainly including where they cut against the paper's own hypotheses and, in one case, against a finding this very paper had already published in an earlier revision, because we would rather a reader trust the honesty of a small preliminary result than doubt the completeness of a larger one.

First, and most immediately strikingly, the seed-to-seed variance is large -- large enough that it changes which conclusions the data actually supports. Demand for \texttt{full} ranges from 0.00\% to 1.60\% across five seeds; for \texttt{single\_objective}, from 0.00\% to 0.62\%. This is a genuine property of training a several-thousand-parameter policy with vanilla REINFORCE for only 500 episodes on a small candidate graph, not an artifact of our reporting, and it is compounded by the run-to-run non-determinism reported below: with five seeds, none of the demand, equity, or coverage differences between \texttt{full}, \texttt{single\_objective}, and \texttt{flat\_encoder} in Table~\ref{tab:results} are large relative to their standard deviations, and we do not claim otherwise.

Second, on the one metric directly comparable in spirit across all five rows -- how much OD demand a 20-station line satisfies -- both non-neural baselines beat every one of our variants, and the genetic algorithm beats everything in the table, including the tabular RL baseline: the GA's mean (2.38\%) exceeds the tabular baseline's (1.36\%), which in turn exceeds our three variants' means (0.16--0.61\%), by a factor of roughly two to fifteen depending on which of our variants a given baseline is compared against. This is not the result we expected going in -- if anything, adding the GA makes the comparison this section has to reckon with more consequential, not less, since it is not only ``does deep RL need to be this complicated'' (\citeauthor{michailidis2026}'s question) but ``does RL, tabular or deep, need to be involved at all, at this problem scale.'' We do not think either finding should be read as evidence against a GNN-based, multi-objective approach in general; both are, we think, substantially explained by features of this specific comparison that will not hold once the method is evaluated as designed. Our variants spend their 500 episodes learning to balance either three objectives under a randomly sampled weight each episode (\texttt{full}, \texttt{flat\_encoder}) or a single objective but through a several-thousand-parameter neural policy rather than a lookup table or a population of candidate lines (\texttt{single\_objective}), while a tabular method with one entry per state-action pair, or a genetic algorithm searching directly over candidate lines with no representation to learn at all, can, on a state-action space as constrained as a single 20-station Xi'an rollout, simply find or memorize good trajectories faster than any of our neural variants can learn a generalizable representation from the same number of samples -- exactly \citeauthor{michailidis2026}'s point, extended by this revision's addition to cover classical search as well as tabular RL, and exactly the pattern we would expect to reverse once the state-action space is large enough (Chengdu) or the network is initialized from a useful prior (the cross-city curriculum, Section~\ref{sec:experiments-curriculum}) rather than trained from scratch on one small city with a high-variance policy-gradient method. We also note, in the interest of not letting a favorable-looking round number stand unexamined, that the GA's budget here (100 population, 50 generations, Section~\ref{sec:experiments}) was chosen for this revision's time constraints rather than tuned for maximum performance, and its demand score was still improving at that budget's end (Appendix~\ref{secA1}); a larger budget would very plausibly widen, not narrow, the gap this paragraph reports.

Third, on equity and coverage -- the two objectives the tabular baseline's own reward formulation does not optimize for at all -- neither the version of this table reported here nor either of its two predecessors agree with each other on which architecture, if any, has an advantage, which we think is itself the more important finding than any one version's specific ranking. An original three-seed draft reported an equity and coverage advantage for \texttt{full} that we had, at that stage, called this paper's most suggestive finding. A five-seed, non-deterministic-GPU version superseding it instead found \texttt{full}'s mean the \emph{lowest} of the three variants on all three metrics -- demand, equity, and coverage alike -- which we reported as a retraction of the three-seed finding. The version reported in Table~\ref{tab:results} above, a five-seed sweep under now-verified deterministic training (and, confounded with that fix, a change from GPU to CPU compute -- Section~\ref{sec:experiments}'s note on Table~\ref{tab:results} explains why we did not attempt to isolate the two), disagrees with \emph{both} predecessors' ranking: \texttt{full} now has the \emph{highest} mean demand ($0.61$, versus $0.16$ and $0.28$) and the \emph{highest} mean coverage ($0.15$, versus $0.10$ and $0.12$) of the three variants, with its equity mean ($0.42$) between \texttt{single\_objective}'s ($0.65$) and \texttt{flat\_encoder}'s ($0.32$). Every one of these gaps, across all three versions of this table, sits well inside one combined standard deviation, and we do not read any of the three rankings as evidence that one architecture is actually better or worse than another. We read the fact that three independent regenerations of the identical comparison -- differing only in seed count, then in determinism and compute backend -- have now produced three qualitatively different rankings as the strongest evidence in this paper that, at this graph size, this episode budget, and this seed count, this specific architectural comparison is not a settled question in any direction, not merely an unlucky single draw we happened to catch. We retract the specific equity-advantage claim from the three-seed version and do not reinstate any directional architectural claim in its place; the underlying architectural and weight-conditioning contributions (Sections~\ref{sec:method-gnn}--\ref{sec:method-policy}) do not depend on any of the three versions' specific ranking. Figure~\ref{fig:xian-map} and its caption describe the non-deterministic-GPU version's seed-42 checkpoint specifically (Section~\ref{sec:experiments} notes this was not regenerated in this revision's deterministic re-run), so it should be read as an illustration of the seed-to-seed variance this section documents, not as a currently-reproducible reference trajectory.

Fourth, Table~\ref{tab:weight-sensitivity} (plotted in Figure~\ref{fig:weight-sensitivity}) confirms the corrected weight-conditioning mechanism produces non-identical output for this one trained checkpoint (seed 42) across the four weight settings, with coverage-only evaluation now producing the \emph{highest} coverage of the four (0.117, versus 0.048--0.050 for the other three) -- the direction we would naively expect, unlike an earlier version of this table (a different, no-longer-reproducible seed-42 training run) which had reported the opposite, counterintuitive pattern. We do not present that reversal as a second confirmation of anything; we present it as a direct illustration of the finding immediately below, since it is the same checkpoint-identity problem (Table~\ref{tab:results}'s footnote) playing out in a second table. We flag explicitly that Table~\ref{tab:weight-sensitivity}, Table~\ref{tab:training-curve}, and Figure~\ref{fig:xian-map} were \emph{not} regenerated under the deterministic configuration in this revision -- only Table~\ref{tab:results}'s main ablation comparison was -- so they describe the earlier, non-deterministic-GPU seed-42 checkpoint, not a currently-reproducible one; a fresh deterministic seed-42 run (Section~\ref{sec:experiments}'s note on Table~\ref{tab:results}) in fact shows a \emph{smaller} degree of weight differentiation than this table reports (three of the four weight settings collapsing to an identical action sequence, with only coverage-only differing), which we did not have time to fold into a fully re-verified replacement of this table, its figure, and the training-curve table without risking exactly the kind of rushed, under-checked update Section~\ref{sec:experiments-verification} warns against.

Fifth, and the most methodologically consequential finding in this revision of the paper: re-running \texttt{full} at seed 42 under otherwise byte-identical configuration (same code, same hyperparameters, same hardware) three separate times during this round of experiments produced three different results (uniform-weight demand/equity/coverage of $0.18\%/0.36/0.10$, $0.00\%/0.20/0.05$, and $0.17\%/0.30/0.09$ respectively) -- not the exact reproduction an earlier draft of this paper had reported and relied on to contrast against Ho Chi Minh City's (Section~\ref{sec:experiments-curriculum}) documented non-determinism. We traced this, as with Ho Chi Minh City, to \texttt{torch\_geometric.utils.scatter}'s non-deterministic CUDA reduction in the GNN encoder's message aggregation (Eq.~\ref{eq:gnn-relations}), which \texttt{scripts/train\_xian.py} does not disable via \texttt{torch.use\_deterministic\_algorithms(True)} any more than \texttt{scripts/train\_hcmc.py} does. Two things follow. First, the earlier claim that Xi'an's training was exactly reproducible while only Ho Chi Minh City's was not (an asymmetry we had reported plainly, in good faith, based on a single spot-check) was itself incorrect, and we correct it here rather than let a stale claim stand uncorrected once contradicted by further testing -- the same verification discipline Section~\ref{sec:experiments-verification} describes generally, applied to our own earlier claim about that discipline's results. Second, and more importantly for how to read this entire section, this non-determinism is large enough in practice to change not just decimal-point details but a headline comparative claim (the equity-advantage reversal in the Third finding above), which reframes how much confidence any single training run -- ours or, we suspect, other unaudited GNN-based MNEP results in this literature -- should be given absent either a full seed sweep or deterministic training. We report this finding itself as a fourth methodological pitfall alongside the two named in Section~\ref{sec1}'s contributions and the third named in Section~\ref{sec:experiments-hcmc}, because we think it is the most generalizable of the four: any GNN-based MNEP method built on \texttt{torch\_geometric}'s default scatter reduction, on either city's graph, inherits this same reproducibility hazard whether or not its authors happen to notice.

Sixth: we implemented and verified the fix rather than only naming it as future work. Enabling \texttt{torch.use\_deterministic\_algorithms(True)} (with \texttt{CUBLAS\_WORKSPACE\_CONFIG} set before CUDA initializes, as PyTorch requires) in both training scripts was necessary but, on first attempt, not sufficient: re-running \texttt{full}/seed-42 twice under this flag still produced different results starting from episode 0, before any gradient update -- surfacing a second, independent non-determinism source we had not previously identified. \texttt{scripts/train\_xian.py}'s training loop calls the Gymnasium environment's \texttt{reset()} once per episode without a seed argument; Gymnasium's environments only reseed their internal RNG when \texttt{reset(seed=\ldots)} is given a non-\texttt{None} value, so an environment created and then repeatedly reset without one draws its starting state from OS entropy on every process launch, independent of \texttt{torch.manual\_seed}. Our own evaluation code had always passed \texttt{seed=args.seed} to \texttt{reset()} (which is why final-evaluation greedy rollouts looked deterministic in isolation), but the training loop itself had not. Seeding the environment once, immediately after construction, is a one-line, low-risk fix (\texttt{env.reset(seed=args.seed)} before the training loop begins; subsequent per-episode resets continue drawing from that now-deterministically-seeded stream). With both fixes applied, four independent re-runs of Xi'an's \texttt{full}/seed-42 configuration, and a further two independent re-runs of Ho Chi Minh City's \texttt{full}/seed-42 configuration, each produced byte-identical demand, equity, and coverage at every logged checkpoint within their respective set, differing only in wall-clock time. In this revision, we used this newly-deterministic configuration to regenerate Table~\ref{tab:results} -- the main, most heavily cited architectural comparison -- with all 15 runs (3 methods $\times$ 5 seeds) completing in well under half an hour on CPU alone, confirming this fix's cost is negligible once implemented; the new numbers, and the fact that they disagree with both this table's own predecessors on which architecture nominally leads, are discussed in the Third finding above. We did not extend this regeneration to Tables~\ref{tab:weight-sensitivity}--\ref{tab:radiation-accessibility}, whose specific checkpoints, figures, and (for Ho Chi Minh City) longer per-run training time made a fully re-verified update to all of them beyond what this revision's remaining time allowed without risking a rushed, under-checked replacement; a full deterministic re-run of the remaining tables is accordingly still future work (Section~\ref{sec13}), though narrower in scope than before this revision.

\subsection{Training dynamics}\label{sec:experiments-dynamics}

The final-episode numbers in Table~\ref{tab:results} are worth checking against the actual training trajectory behind them, since a converged final value and an unstable or non-monotonic path to it tell a reader different things about how much to trust that final value as representative. Table~\ref{tab:training-curve} (plotted in Figure~\ref{fig:training-curve}) reports the specific \texttt{full} (seed 42) checkpoint behind Table~\ref{tab:weight-sensitivity}'s demand, equity, and coverage at five points across its 500-episode training run -- and, consistent with the reproducibility finding above, is itself just one of several different training curves the identical configuration produced when re-run, not a fixed reference trajectory. This particular run's most immediately visible feature is a degenerate episode 0: the untrained policy's first rollout satisfies exactly zero demand, which trivially (Eq.~\ref{eq:gini}) scores a maximal equity of 1.000 -- the same zero-demand-implies-trivial-equity artifact Section~\ref{sec:experiments-curriculum} documents for Ho Chi Minh City's episode-0 checkpoints, now shown to appear on Xi'an's grid too rather than being specific to Ho Chi Minh City's irregular graph. Demand recovers to a modest 0.34--0.43\% range by episode 20 and stays there, non-monotonically, for the remainder of training. What the checkpoint table supports is a claim we make plainly rather than soften: none of the three objectives shows a clean, monotonic learning curve across 500 episodes on this seed, equity drifts downward from its trivial episode-0 maximum without resettling on a clearly better value, and coverage, after the degenerate episode-0 start, stays within a narrow band (0.128--0.133) for the remaining checkpoints, all consistent with -- not contradicting -- this section's repeated characterization of 500 episodes as insufficient for full convergence on a several-thousand-parameter policy trained with unbatched, high-variance REINFORCE, now further compounded by the run-to-run non-determinism reported above.

\subsection{Ho Chi Minh City: a second, structurally distinct case study}\label{sec:experiments-hcmc}

Ho Chi Minh City is the second city in the evaluation programme described in Section~\ref{sec:data}, chosen to run next specifically because -- unlike Xi'an's uniform, previously-benchmarked grid -- it forces the method to operate on the kind of graph its motivating cities (Section~\ref{sec1}) actually have: 322 irregular commune/ward polygons of varying size and shape, a queen-contiguity adjacency structurally distinct from OD-flow connectivity (Section~\ref{sec:method-implementation}), and an OD matrix disaggregated from a real household travel survey (Eq.~\ref{eq:od-disaggregation}) rather than a benchmark's ready-made grid. We report the same \texttt{full}/\texttt{single\_objective}/\texttt{flat\_encoder} ablation design as Table~\ref{tab:results}, trained under the matched 500-episode, 20-station budget, five seeds (1, 2, 3, 7, 42) each.

\textbf{A third implementation pitfall: single-start greedy evaluation is not robust on an irregular graph.} Our first attempt at evaluating a trained Ho Chi Minh City policy reused Table~\ref{tab:results}'s evaluation protocol unchanged: roll the trained policy out greedily from one fixed starting zone. Two of nine seed/ablation combinations then reported exactly zero demand satisfied -- not low demand, zero -- which on inspection was not a training failure: those starting zones had 10 and 42 OD-connected neighbors respectively, ruling out an isolated-start explanation. The actual cause is structural, not a bug in the usual sense: Xi'an's grid-walk action space makes geographic adjacency and OD connectivity nearly the same relation by construction (a compass-neighbor move is very likely also an OD-connected move, since the grid discretizes a small city uniformly), but Ho Chi Minh City's queen-contiguity adjacency (mean degree $\sim$6, verified directly against the built graph) and OD-flow connectivity (mean degree $\sim$40, median 32, up to 197) are two genuinely different relations over the same 322 zones -- not merely different in degree but, since the two starting zones that produced zero-demand rollouts had 10 and 42 OD-connected neighbors respectively, evidently not reducible to ``low-OD-degree zones are the problem'' either, so a 19-step greedy rollout from one fixed contiguous-neighbor walk can, for some starting zones, never step onto an OD-connected pair at all. We fixed this not by discarding the affected runs but by changing the evaluation protocol itself: every Ho Chi Minh City result below averages greedy rollouts from five random starting zones per weight setting (\texttt{scripts/train\_hcmc.py}'s \texttt{greedy\_eval}), rather than one fixed zone -- the same class of pitfall Section~\ref{sec:experiments}'s Bugs 1 and 2 already warned about: a method that trains and evaluates without error on one city's action-space geometry can encode an assumption (here, contiguity $\approx$ OD-connectivity) that a structurally different city's graph silently violates.

Two findings stand out at five seeds, reported with the same seed-variance caveats as Table~\ref{tab:results} -- and, per the reproducibility finding there, an additional caveat that a fresh re-run of any of these configurations is not guaranteed to reproduce the specific numbers below either. First, and most strikingly different from Xi'an: \texttt{flat\_encoder} collapses to near-zero demand on Ho Chi Minh City's graph across all five seeds ($0.19 \pm 0.29\%$, versus \texttt{full}'s $2.83\%$ and \texttt{single\_objective}'s $4.07\%$), a far starker and more consistent gap than the encoder ablation showed on Xi'an's grid, where the fuller five-seed picture (Section~\ref{sec:experiments}) shows \texttt{flat\_encoder}'s mean demand ($0.39 \pm 0.46\%$) if anything exceeding \texttt{full}'s own ($0.18 \pm 0.37\%$) rather than trailing behind it. We read Ho Chi Minh City's collapse as evidence that message passing over the contiguity/OD-flow relations (Section~\ref{sec:method-gnn}) is doing genuinely more work on an irregular graph where those two relations are structurally distinct than on a grid where they nearly coincide -- exactly the property Section~\ref{sec:method-gnn} argued a flat feature vector cannot represent -- but we are explicit that this same ablation shows the \emph{opposite} directional pattern on Xi'an, which is itself a useful caution against generalizing an encoder ablation's effect from one city's graph to another's without checking. Second, \texttt{full} and \texttt{single\_objective} are not distinguishably different on demand or coverage given seed variance this large, but \texttt{full}'s equity mean is directionally higher ($0.84$ versus $0.72$, a gap that holds up at five seeds where it did not for the analogous Xi'an comparison in Table~\ref{tab:results}) -- we still report this as suggestive rather than established, precisely because Xi'an's now-retracted equity finding is a direct demonstration of how unreliable a suggestive-looking gap at this seed count can turn out to be.

We also confirmed the weight-conditioning mechanism (Section~\ref{sec:method-policy}) remains active on Ho Chi Minh City's graph -- it does not silently regress to the Bug 2 failure mode -- but its \emph{degree} of differentiation across weight settings is visibly weaker than Table~\ref{tab:weight-sensitivity}'s Xi'an result: evaluating the \texttt{scratch}-condition seed-42 policy from Section~\ref{sec:experiments-curriculum} below (a separately-trained \texttt{full}-architecture checkpoint at the same seed, not the numerically distinct seed-42 checkpoint behind Table~\ref{tab:hcmc-results}'s row -- see the reproducibility caveat in Section~\ref{sec:experiments-curriculum} for why these two same-seed runs differ) greedily under all four weightings after 500 episodes, three of the four (uniform, equity-only, coverage-only) converge to an identical action sequence and identical scores, with only the demand-only weighting producing a distinguishably different line. We do not read this as evidence the mechanism is broken -- a control run with \texttt{--train\_episodes 0} confirms the four weightings still produce different outputs at random initialization, so the pathway from $\mathbf{w}$ to the policy's decision is intact -- but rather as a genuine training-dynamics finding distinct in character from Xi'an's: after 500 episodes, the trained Ho Chi Minh City policy becomes confident enough in one dominant greedy path that small weight-driven perturbations to the pooled query (Eq.~\ref{eq:policy-score}) no longer flip the arg max action at most steps, even though the underlying representation remains weight-sensitive. Whether this reflects Ho Chi Minh City's specific graph structure, undertraining relative to Xi'an, or something about the irregular action space collapsing the effective decision surface faster is a question this single result cannot settle, and we flag it as exactly that -- an open question, not a claim -- rather than either over- or understating what one seed's behavioral check shows.

\subsection{Validating the coverage proxy against the true radiation model, for the one city where that is possible}\label{sec:experiments-radiation}

Section~\ref{sec:problem} and Section~\ref{sec:experiments}'s Metrics subsection both flag the Chebyshev-distance coverage proxy (Eq.~\ref{eq:coverage}) as a stand-in for \citeauthor{zhang2024}'s radiation-model accessibility measure \citep{simini2012}, not the measure itself, and Section~\ref{sec:discussion}'s Limitations name implementing the true radiation model as near-term, tractable follow-up work. Xi'an's benchmark grid (Section~\ref{sec:data}) has no real population data or geographic coordinates attached to it -- it is an abstract grid-index environment with no confirmed geo-reference -- so the radiation model is not computable there without fabricating a population surface, which we will not do. Ho Chi Minh City is different: the real CSL\_HCMC dataset (Section~\ref{sec:data}) ships each zone's actual 2019 population (\texttt{Pop\_2019}) and real projected centroid coordinates, which is exactly what Simini et al.'s formula needs. We implemented it directly (\texttt{src/data/hcmc.py::build\_radiation\_probabilities}, unit-tested against hand-computed values on a small synthetic example) and used it to evaluate, post-hoc, the same trained Ho Chi Minh City checkpoints (\texttt{full}, \texttt{single\_objective}, \texttt{flat\_encoder}, 3 seeds each, with weights saved via \texttt{--save\_checkpoint}) behind Table~\ref{tab:hcmc-results} -- a read-only evaluation, not a change to the training reward, since re-deriving the reward and re-verifying the Tchebycheff scalarization against a third real objective is a larger undertaking than this submission's remaining scope allows and we say so rather than rush it.

For a built network $V_t$, we score each candidate region by the radiation model's flow probability to its single best-connected built station, averaged over all regions (\texttt{radiation\_accessibility\_score}, bounded in $[0,1]$ by construction, the same closest-built-station form as the coverage proxy but continuous and population-weighted rather than a hard within-$K$-hops indicator) -- full details in Appendix~\ref{secA1}.

The honest result is a null one, and we report it as such rather than search for a more flattering cut of the data: all three ablations' radiation-accessibility means sit within roughly one standard deviation of each other, showing none of the separation Table~\ref{tab:hcmc-results}'s coverage column shows between \texttt{flat\_encoder} ($0.222$) and the other two variants ($0.230$, $0.247$) -- visible directly in Figure~\ref{fig:radiation-vs-coverage}'s two panels, plotted on matched axes for exactly this comparison. This directly tests, for the first time in this paper, whether the coverage proxy's architectural sensitivity actually reflects something the true accessibility measure would also show, or whether it is an artifact of the specific geometry the proxy rewards (Section~\ref{sec:discussion}'s Threats to validity had already named this as a construct-validity risk, on the strength of qualitative reasoning alone); the answer, on this evidence, is closer to the latter than we had hoped going in. We see two non-exclusive explanations, and are explicit that we cannot fully distinguish between them with the evidence in hand: either the radiation model's population-weighted flow probability is genuinely much less sensitive to which 20 of 322 zones get built than the coverage proxy's geographic-spread indicator is, because a station's population-weighted accessibility contribution is dominated by a handful of very-high-population zones regardless of the specific architecture that selected the other 19 stations; or 20 stations out of 322 candidate zones (a 6\% selection rate) is simply too sparse a network for any of the three architectures' differences to leave a detectable mark on a population-aggregated accessibility measure at this scale. Either way, this is a genuine, if modest, validation finding rather than the confirmatory check we had expected to run: implementing the true radiation model did not simply corroborate the coverage proxy's already-reported architectural ranking, and a reader should weight Table~\ref{tab:hcmc-results}'s coverage column accordingly -- as evidence about geographic spread specifically, not as a verified stand-in for population-weighted accessibility in general.

\textbf{A fifth implementation pitfall: an incomplete checkpoint.} Producing this table surfaced one more methodological finding worth naming explicitly, alongside the four already reported in this paper. \texttt{scripts/train\_hcmc.py}'s \texttt{--save\_checkpoint} originally saved only the policy, weight-embedding, and GNN message-passing-layer parameters -- \emph{not} the encoder's input projection (\texttt{HeteroGNNEncoder.input\_proj}, or, for the \texttt{flat\_encoder} ablation, the entirety of that encoder's own \texttt{input\_proj}, its only parameter), on the design rationale that the input projection is city-specific and therefore correctly excluded from the Xi'an-to-Ho-Chi-Minh-City curriculum transfer (Section~\ref{sec:experiments-curriculum}) that motivated adding checkpoint-saving in the first place. That rationale is valid for the cross-city case; it silently produces a wrong result for a same-city post-hoc evaluation: any script loading a saved checkpoint to evaluate the trained model on its \emph{own} city -- this radiation-accessibility script, and the map figure below -- was reconstructing the policy and message-passing weights correctly but leaving the input projection at a fresh random initialization, never the trained one. We caught this only when a review of Figure~\ref{fig:hcmc-map} found that two independent re-runs of the map-generation script produced two different station sets from what was supposed to be the same fixed checkpoint -- initially indistinguishable from the GPU non-determinism this paper already documents at length, until tracing it further showed the discrepancy was present even under the deterministic configuration (Section~\ref{sec:experiments}), which ruled that out and pointed to the missing state instead. We fixed \texttt{save\_checkpoint} to also store the input projection, retrained the nine checkpoints behind this table under the identical configuration (same code, hyperparameters, and seeds as those behind Table~\ref{tab:hcmc-results}, though not, per the non-determinism finding above, guaranteed to be bit-identical to those specific runs), and verified the map-generation script now reproduces byte-identically across independent re-runs. The corrected numbers above replace an earlier version of this table computed with the incomplete checkpoints; the qualitative finding -- no architectural separation on the true radiation measure -- is unchanged between the two, which is itself a small reassurance that this specific bug was not driving the null result, but we report the correction anyway because reporting only the version of a bug hunt that happened not to change the headline finding would be exactly the kind of selective disclosure this paper argues against elsewhere.

\subsection{Cross-city curriculum: Xi'an to Ho Chi Minh City}\label{sec:experiments-curriculum}

With Xi'an and Ho Chi Minh City both trained, Algorithm~\ref{alg:curriculum}'s cross-city curriculum can, for the first time in this paper, actually be measured rather than only motivated. For each of the three seeds, we ran Ho Chi Minh City training twice under otherwise identical conditions (500 episodes, 20 stations, same seed): once from a random initialization (\texttt{scratch}), and once warm-started from the correspondingly-seeded Xi'an \texttt{full} checkpoint (\texttt{curriculum}) -- transferring the GNN encoder's message-passing weights, the policy/value heads, and the weight-embedding layer (Section~\ref{sec:method-policy}) wholesale, then continuing training on Ho Chi Minh City's graph for the same 500-episode budget.

\textbf{Evidence of genuine positive transfer at initialization.} The cleanest test of whether anything actually transferred is the very first Ho Chi Minh City episode, before any city-specific gradient update has been applied: a policy with no useful prior produces close to a random walk over the feasibility mask, while a policy carrying real, transferred structure should not. In two of three seeds, this is exactly what we see: at episode 0, the \texttt{curriculum} policy already satisfies non-zero demand (seed 42: $0.253\%$; seed 1: $1.033\%$) where the from-\texttt{scratch} policy satisfies none at all ($0.000\%$ in both cases); the third seed ties at zero for both. This asymmetry cannot be explained by anything other than the transferred weights, since episode 0 is evaluated before any Ho Chi Minh-City-specific learning has occurred, and it is, we think, the most direct evidence in this paper that the GNN encoder's learned message-passing structure is not Xi'an-specific in the way a tabular method's lookup table necessarily would be (Section~\ref{sec:method-curriculum}). It also surfaces a metric-interpretation subtlety worth naming explicitly, in the same spirit as Table~\ref{tab:weight-sensitivity}'s coverage-weighted finding: the \texttt{scratch} policy's episode-0 equity score is a perfect $1.0$ in all three seeds, which looks, out of context, like an enviable starting point, but is in fact the Gini index's degenerate value for a network that satisfies exactly zero demand for every population group equally -- a policy that helps no one is trivially, uselessly ``equitable'' by Eq.~\ref{eq:gini}, and this is exactly the failure mode that makes reporting demand and equity together, never equity alone, essential throughout this paper.

\textbf{No established final-performance advantage after equal continued training.} Table~\ref{tab:curriculum-results} shows \texttt{curriculum}'s mean demand directionally higher than \texttt{scratch}'s ($3.45\%$ versus $2.57\%$) and equity and coverage essentially tied, but with only three seeds and standard deviations this large relative to the means, none of these differences are statistically distinguishable, and we do not claim otherwise. This is, on reflection, close to the result the experimental design should be expected to produce, and worth stating precisely rather than reading as a negative result about transfer: both conditions received the \emph{same} 500-episode training budget on Ho Chi Minh City, so this comparison tests whether warm-starting improves the \emph{asymptotic quality} reached after a fixed, generous training budget, not whether it improves \emph{sample efficiency} -- the actual claim Section~\ref{sec:method-curriculum} and \citeauthor{michailidis2026}'s efficiency critique are about. A policy that starts from a better initialization but is given just as many further gradient steps as one starting from scratch has every opportunity to converge to a similar place either way; the informative test is whether \texttt{curriculum} reaches \texttt{scratch}'s final performance in meaningfully \emph{fewer} episodes -- which we test directly below, rather than leaving as a stated-but-unmeasured next step.

\textbf{Testing the actual sample-efficiency claim under a reduced episode budget.} We re-ran the identical \texttt{scratch}-versus-\texttt{curriculum} comparison at a fifth of the original training budget (100 episodes instead of 500), for the same three seeds, to test whether warm-starting reaches usable performance in \emph{fewer} episodes rather than only a comparable one under a matched, generous budget.

This is the clearest evidence for the curriculum's sample-efficiency claim in this paper, and it is worth stating exactly what it does and does not show rather than overselling it (Figure~\ref{fig:curriculum} plots both this table and Table~\ref{tab:curriculum-results} side by side). On coverage, the result is unambiguous and consistent across all three seeds without exception: \texttt{curriculum} exceeds \texttt{scratch} at every seed under this reduced budget ($0.2801$ vs.\ $0.2534$; $0.2863$ vs.\ $0.2627$; $0.3075$ vs.\ $0.2435$), a pattern no seed contradicts. On demand, the picture is real but less uniform: in two of three seeds, warm-starting reaches non-trivial demand satisfaction in 100 episodes ($2.29\%$ and $1.72\%$) where training from scratch reaches essentially none ($0.00\%$ in both), a roughly fifty-fold difference in the aggregate mean ($1.34\%$ versus $0.03\%$); the third seed (42) is the exception, where \texttt{curriculum} actually trails \texttt{scratch} slightly on demand ($0.004\%$ versus $0.077\%$, both still small in absolute terms). We report this with the exact statistic the small sample size actually supports rather than only a qualitative read of the pattern: with $n=3$ paired seeds, an exact one-sided sign test against the null of no systematic direction gives $p=0.125$ for coverage's 3-of-3 win and $p=0.5$ for demand's 2-of-3 win -- the coverage result is directionally consistent but neither reaches conventional significance at this sample size, and demand's is weaker still. We therefore read this as genuine, if imperfect and not yet statistically established, evidence that cross-city warm-starting lets Ho Chi Minh City's policy reach useful performance in a fraction of the episodes from-scratch training needs -- exactly the sample-efficiency claim Section~\ref{sec:method-curriculum} motivates the curriculum with, and the reduced-budget follow-up we had, until this revision, only promised rather than delivered -- while being explicit that it is not a uniform win across every seed and that three seeds cannot, on its own, distinguish a real effect from a pattern consistent with chance, consistent with everything else in this paper's honestly high seed-to-seed variance.

\textbf{A caveat on exact reproducibility.} One of the three Ho Chi Minh City \texttt{scratch} runs behind Table~\ref{tab:hcmc-results} and Table~\ref{tab:curriculum-results} did not reproduce across independently launched runs: seed 42's \texttt{full}-ablation run (Table~\ref{tab:hcmc-results}) and its otherwise-identically-configured \texttt{scratch} run in this curriculum comparison (same code, same seed, same hyperparameters, run as separate processes) produced different final scores ($8.66\%$ demand versus $4.80\%$), while seeds 1 and 2 reproduced exactly in both. We traced this to \texttt{torch\_geometric.utils.scatter}'s CUDA reduction (Eq.~\ref{eq:gnn-relations}'s message aggregation, \texttt{src/gnn/encoder.py}), which is not guaranteed bit-for-bit deterministic on GPU without \texttt{torch.use\_deterministic\_algorithms(True)} -- not set in either training script at the time this table was collected -- because concurrent CUDA threads can accumulate floating-point sums in a run-to-run-varying order. We had originally attributed this specifically to Ho Chi Minh City's larger, denser OD-flow relation (13{,}042 edges, versus Xi'an's much smaller grid) giving the non-determinism more opportunity to compound, on the strength of a single earlier spot-check that had found Xi'an's seed-42 run reproducing exactly; Section~\ref{sec:experiments}'s Ablations subsection reports that this attribution does not hold once Xi'an is checked more than once -- re-running Xi'an's identical \texttt{full}, seed-42 configuration three times during this round of experiments produced three different results, not the one exact reproduction we had generalized from. The non-determinism is real on both cities' graphs; graph size may still affect how much it compounds, but graph size is not why one city reproduced and the other did not, since neither reliably does. Every comparison in Table~\ref{tab:hcmc-results} and Table~\ref{tab:curriculum-results} is between runs launched back-to-back within the same script invocation for a controlled comparison (\texttt{scratch} vs.\ \texttt{curriculum} at matched seed), never across separately-launched runs, specifically because of this finding. We subsequently identified and fixed the full root cause -- \texttt{torch.use\_deterministic\_algorithms(True)} plus, on Xi'an, seeding the Gymnasium environment once at construction (Section~\ref{sec:experiments} reports both) -- and confirmed byte-identical re-runs on both cities' graphs, but did not regenerate this table under it given the scope already covered in this revision.

\subsection{Training cost and a real, measured carbon comparison}\label{sec:experiments-carbon}

Section~\ref{sec:experiments} flagged a formal CO$_2$ accounting following \citeauthor{michailidis2026}'s method as deferred work, but the tabular baseline's own runs were, in fact, tracked with the same carbon-accounting tool (\texttt{codecarbon}) their paper uses, as a byproduct of running their code as-is (Appendix~\ref{secA1}); we report those real, measured numbers here rather than withholding them until our own method is instrumented identically. Across the 5 seed runs behind Table~\ref{tab:results}'s tabular RL row, \texttt{codecarbon} recorded a mean of 0.72\,g CO$_2$eq and 1.52\,Wh per 500-episode training run, completing in a mean wall-clock time of 96.3\,s on CPU alone -- for a method with no GPU requirement at all, this is about as close to a rounding error as a machine-learning training run's carbon footprint gets. Our own three variants, trained on a single NVIDIA RTX A2000 laptop GPU under the identical 500-episode, 20-station budget, took a mean of 193.9\,s (\texttt{full}), 199.1\,s (\texttt{single\_objective}), and 210.0\,s (\texttt{flat\_encoder}) wall-clock -- roughly double the tabular baseline's CPU-only time, despite running on dedicated GPU hardware. We have not yet instrumented our own runs with \texttt{codecarbon} to convert this into a directly comparable emissions figure, and say so rather than estimate one from wall-clock time and a GPU's rated power draw, which would overstate the precision of what is actually a rough approximation; what the wall-clock comparison alone already shows plainly, though, is that at Xi'an's scale our current implementation is not GPU-bound in any way that would make the hardware pay for itself -- a single 20-step episode's GNN forward pass and policy update is small enough that per-episode overhead (Python/environment interaction, unbatched rollouts) likely dominates actual GPU compute time, which is exactly the kind of inefficiency that batching rollouts across an entire city (or, eventually, across the cross-city curriculum's multiple cities at once) is designed to amortize away, and exactly the kind of inefficiency a single small-city, single-environment result like this one cannot yet demonstrate has been solved. This is, if anything, a second independent line of evidence -- alongside the raw demand comparison above -- for taking \citeauthor{michailidis2026}'s efficiency critique seriously at Xi'an's scale rather than assuming a GNN's architectural sophistication justifies its cost by default. Comparing the matched \texttt{scratch}-versus-\texttt{curriculum} pairs from Section~\ref{sec:experiments-curriculum} directly (same seed, same script invocation, back-to-back), \texttt{curriculum} took longer in all three seeds -- 90.0\,s versus 37.2\,s (seed 42), 60.7\,s versus 36.6\,s (seed 1), 111.8\,s versus 38.6\,s (seed 2), a 1.7--2.9$\times$ slowdown with no exceptions. Since both conditions perform the identical number of forward/backward passes per episode and differ only in the one-time cost of loading a checkpoint at the start of training, a cost that small cannot explain a 2--3$\times$ per-run difference; we do not have a confirmed explanation for this and are explicit that we do not, rather than reach for a plausible-sounding one, and flag it as a genuine open question rather than dismiss it as scheduling noise, particularly because the direction is consistent across all three seeds rather than scattered as pure noise would tend to be. This is a real limitation on how strongly Section~\ref{sec:experiments-curriculum}'s transfer-learning evidence should be read as an unqualified efficiency win, and we say so plainly rather than let a wall-clock number sit uncommented next to a section arguing for the value of the curriculum it measures. We have not instrumented \texttt{codecarbon} for Ho Chi Minh City either, for the same reason stated above for Xi'an.

\subsection{Verification practices}\label{sec:experiments-verification}

Because this paper reports five implementation pitfalls in total (two of them, central to this section's Xi'an narrative specifically, caught only through deliberate, active verification rather than through the training process itself looking wrong), it is worth being explicit about the verification practices behind every other number reported above, so a reader can calibrate how much confidence to place in claims we have \emph{not} flagged as bugs. The codebase underlying this paper carries 54 unit tests covering the GNN encoder (message-passing correctness, gradient flow, CUDA execution, empty-edge handling), the multi-objective reward module (Tchebycheff scalarization against hand-computed values, weight-simplex sampling coverage), the prioritized replay buffer (priority-weighted sampling distributions verified over 500 draws), the policy/value head (masked-softmax correctness, gradient flow through sampled actions), and the HCMC data pipeline (contiguity-edge construction against a hand-built toy grid, the OD disaggregation fix against the real district-name mismatch described above) -- all passing at the time of writing, and re-run before every commit referenced in this paper's accompanying repository. Beyond automated testing, every claim in Section~\ref{sec:data} about data source availability, licensing, and content was verified by directly cloning and inspecting the underlying repository or shapefile, not merely by locating a description of it; several claims from an earlier research pass turned out to need correction on direct inspection (Section~\ref{sec:data}), which is itself evidence for why this stricter verification standard was applied throughout rather than only where a problem happened to be suspected in advance. The manuscript itself went through several rounds of independent adversarial review -- each round conducted with no memory of, or deference to, the previous round's conclusions, explicitly instructed to re-verify factual and numerical claims against primary sources rather than accept plausible-sounding prose. It is worth being precise about what this process did and did not catch, since conflating the two would itself be a small version of the overclaiming this paper argues against elsewhere. The two implementation bugs central to this section's narrative were \emph{not} caught by review: as stated above, one was caught by the code crashing during ordinary use, the other by a deliberate behavioral sanity check we built into the training script specifically because we suspected exactly this class of failure was possible. What review \emph{did} catch, across an initial round on the pre-experiment manuscript (a data-loss bug in the HCMC pipeline, a misattributed citation, an out-of-scope framing error) and a later round on the experimental expansion described in this section, is a set of internal-consistency errors introduced while writing this section up -- incorrect values in Table~\ref{tab:results} traced back to picking the wrong evaluation row from a raw log for one cell, an abstract that described the tabular baseline as ``reimplemented'' when Section~\ref{sec:experiments} states plainly it is the original authors' code run directly, and, at one point, a draft of this very paragraph that had incorrectly attributed the two implementation bugs' discovery to the review process itself. We report that correction here, in the paragraph it corrects, rather than editing it away silently, because a reader recalibrating trust in this section benefits more from seeing that this kind of error is caught and fixed than from a version of the text that never shows it happened.

\subsection{Discussion}\label{sec:discussion}

This paper set out to close three gaps in the MNEP literature -- no method combining GNN spatial representation with multi-objective fairness, no test of whether tabular RL's efficiency advantage holds at scale extremes, and no test of whether learned expansion policies transfer across cities -- and it is important to be precise about which of the three these now-two-city (Xi'an, Ho Chi Minh City) results actually speak to, and which remain open.

\textbf{On the first gap}, the result is genuinely informative, though mixed in a different and more consequential way than an earlier draft of this section reported. A five-seed sweep of Table~\ref{tab:results} finds no reliable equity or coverage advantage for \texttt{full} over either ablation on Xi'an; an earlier, three-seed version of this table had reported one and we had called it this paper's most suggestive finding, a characterization the fuller data does not support and that we retract explicitly (Section~\ref{sec:experiments}). What the fuller sweep establishes instead, and what we think is a more durable contribution than the retracted finding was, is that GPU training non-determinism on the shared GNN encoder is severe enough to invert which architectural variant a given metric appears to favor -- a reproducibility hazard for anyone comparing GNN-based MNEP architectures across a small number of trained runs, in this literature or elsewhere, that only surfaced because we re-ran an identical configuration multiple times rather than trusting one favorable-looking result. Table~\ref{tab:weight-sensitivity} demonstrates, independent of the above and after fixing a real bug that would otherwise have made this claim false, that a single trained policy meaningfully varies its behavior across the demand/equity/coverage trade-off space rather than collapsing to one fixed compromise -- though even this finding is now qualified: two of five Xi'an \texttt{full} seeds (Section~\ref{sec:experiments}) collapse to weight-insensitive behavior after training, mirroring a pattern Section~\ref{sec:experiments-hcmc} already documents for Ho Chi Minh City, so the mechanism is architecturally present and demonstrably influential (Section~\ref{sec:method-policy}) without reliably producing differentiated behavior in every trained instance. Section~\ref{sec:experiments-hcmc}'s Ho Chi Minh City result is where a genuine equity signal survives at five seeds ($0.84$ versus \texttt{single\_objective}'s $0.72$) and the encoder ablation's contribution is starker still, collapsing to near-zero demand rather than only trailing behind -- so the value of message passing and of the multi-objective reward is real and city-dependent, not uniformly present or uniformly absent across the two cities tested so far.

\textbf{On the second gap} -- whether tabular RL's efficiency advantage holds at scale -- this Xi'an result, if anything, is a data point \emph{for} \citeauthor{michailidis2026}'s position rather than against it, and this revision's addition of a genetic-algorithm baseline (Table~\ref{tab:results}) makes the point more sharply than the tabular comparison alone did: under a matched, modest training budget on a mid-sized city, both non-neural baselines beat ours, and a classical search method with no learned representation at all beats the tabular RL baseline too. We think this is the honest and expected outcome at Xi'an's scale, and we say so plainly rather than searching for a more favorable metric to headline, because the paper's actual contribution to this question is a hypothesis worth testing, not a result yet: that both non-neural baselines' advantage should narrow, and eventually reverse, as the state-action space grows toward Chengdu's scale, where a lookup table's implicit reliance on each state-action pair being visited often enough to be estimated reliably becomes harder to satisfy, where a genetic algorithm's population-based search over whole candidate lines becomes a proportionally smaller sample of an exponentially larger combinatorial space, and where a GNN's ability to generalize across structurally similar but never-exactly-visited candidate regions should start to pay for itself. Testing that hypothesis is the single most important remaining experiment in this research programme, not a minor extension of it -- and this revision's finding that even a classical, decades-old search method outperforms our neural approach at this scale, not only a specialized tabular RL method, raises the bar that hypothesis needs to clear.

\textbf{On the third gap}, transfer across cities, this paper now reports a real, honestly-hedged result rather than none at all (Section~\ref{sec:experiments-curriculum}): warm-starting Ho Chi Minh City's policy from Xi'an's trained \texttt{full} checkpoint produces non-zero demand satisfaction at the very first, pre-gradient-update episode in two of three seeds, where training from scratch produces none -- direct evidence that the GNN encoder's learned message-passing structure carries something genuinely city-agnostic, not merely city-specific weights that happen not to hurt. Under the original, matched 500-episode budget this did not yet establish the efficiency claim Section~\ref{sec:method-curriculum} actually motivates the curriculum with, since a matched budget tests asymptotic quality, not whether warm-starting reaches a comparable result in \emph{fewer} episodes; re-running the identical comparison under a fifth of that budget (Table~\ref{tab:reduced-budget-curriculum}) is the direct test of that claim, and finds warm-starting reaching non-trivial demand and higher coverage than training from scratch in most seeds -- a genuine, if not seed-uniform, sample-efficiency result, and the closest this paper comes to directly answering \citeauthor{michailidis2026}'s efficiency critique on its own terms rather than only motivating a response to it architecturally.

\textbf{Limitations.} Beyond the incomplete city coverage already discussed, six limitations of this result deserve explicit statement rather than being left implicit. First, and now the most consequential: training on both cities' graphs exhibits non-deterministic GPU scatter-reduction in the shared GNN encoder's message aggregation (Section~\ref{sec:experiments}, Section~\ref{sec:experiments-curriculum}) severe enough that re-running an identical configuration can change which architectural variant a given metric appears to favor, as it did for Xi'an's equity comparison between an earlier three-seed draft of this paper and the current five-seed one; every quantitative comparison in this paper is between runs controlled for this (matched seeds launched within the same script invocation, never compared across separately-launched runs). We identified and fixed the root cause during this revision -- \texttt{torch.use\_deterministic\_algorithms(True)} plus, on Xi'an specifically, seeding the Gymnasium environment once at construction rather than relying on its default reset behavior (Section~\ref{sec:experiments}) -- and confirmed it produces byte-identical re-runs on both cities' graphs. Table~\ref{tab:results}, the main Xi'an architectural comparison, was regenerated under this fix in this revision and disagrees with both of its own predecessors on which architecture nominally leads (Section~\ref{sec:experiments}), which we read as evidence for genuine unresolved variance rather than a settled correction; the remaining tables (Table~\ref{tab:weight-sensitivity} onward) were not regenerated and should still be read with the seed-to-seed and run-to-run caveats they were originally reported with. Second, this is now a two-city result (Xi'an, Ho Chi Minh City) rather than a single-city one, but Hanoi and Chengdu -- the paper's small-emerging-system and scale-extreme cases, respectively -- remain untested; Ho Chi Minh City's irregular, polygon-based candidate-region graph (Section~\ref{sec:data}) is a structurally different but formally equivalent action-space realization (Section~\ref{sec:method-implementation}) to Xi'an's grid, and exercising it end-to-end surfaced a real evaluation pitfall (Section~\ref{sec:experiments-hcmc}) that Xi'an's more regular graph never would have -- itself evidence for why generalizing across structurally different cities, not only training on more of them, is a meaningful part of validating this method, and why Hanoi's still-untested, even sparser graph should not be assumed to behave like either of the two cities tested so far. Third, our accessibility metric is a Chebyshev-distance coverage proxy for Xi'an, whose benchmark grid has no real population data to compute \citeauthor{zhang2024}'s radiation-model measure from at all; for Ho Chi Minh City, where real population and coordinates make the true radiation model computable, we implemented and evaluated it directly (Section~\ref{sec:experiments-radiation}) and found a null result -- the coverage proxy's architectural sensitivity across ablations does not carry over to the true measure, which we report as a genuine construct-validity finding rather than the confirmatory check we had expected, and a reason to weight the coverage-based comparisons throughout this paper more cautiously than we did before running this check. Fourth, the MetroGNN-like and Zhang-et-al-like baseline rows are principled ablations of our own architecture, not reproductions of either paper's own reported numbers on their own data and code; the comparison this licenses is about the marginal value of each design choice within our method, not a head-to-head claim that our reimplementation of \citeauthor{su2024}'s idea outperforms their published system. Fifth, the reduced-budget cross-city curriculum test (Section~\ref{sec:experiments-curriculum}) shows a consistent coverage advantage for warm-starting across all three seeds tested but a demand advantage in only two of three, with the third seed showing a small reversal; three seeds is not enough to know whether that reversal is a stable minority pattern or noise, and the curriculum, radiation-accessibility, and reduced-budget evaluations all still use three seeds (1, 2, 42) rather than the five-seed sweep now used for the two cities' main ablation tables (Tables~\ref{tab:results} and~\ref{tab:hcmc-results}), for the practical reason that each of those evaluations depends on a specific saved Xi'an checkpoint per seed rather than a fresh from-scratch run. Sixth, the weight-conditioning mechanism, while architecturally present and demonstrably influential at random initialization (confirmed via an untrained-policy control), collapses to weight-insensitive behavior after 500 episodes of training in a substantial minority of trained instances on both cities' graphs (two of five Xi'an \texttt{full} seeds; a majority of weight settings for at least one Ho Chi Minh City checkpoint, Section~\ref{sec:experiments-hcmc}) -- a genuine, only partially understood training-dynamics limitation of training this mechanism with 500 episodes of vanilla REINFORCE, not a claim that the mechanism does not work at all.

None of these limitations, we think, undermine the paper's central methodological contributions -- the architecture, the weight-conditioning mechanism and the bug it fixes, the two concrete implementation pitfalls documented in Section~\ref{sec:experiments} -- which stand independently of how many cities and seeds have been run so far. They do mean that the scale-dependent and transfer-learning claims motivating this research programme (Section~\ref{sec1}) remain hypotheses this paper has argued for and instrumented cleanly, rather than results this paper has already established.

\textbf{Threats to validity.} It is worth organizing the above more formally, in the terms empirical software-engineering and machine-learning evaluation studies typically use, so a reader can weigh each concern against the kind of claim it threatens rather than treating ``limitations'' as an undifferentiated list. \emph{Internal validity} -- whether the reported effects are actually caused by the mechanisms we attribute them to -- is threatened primarily by the GPU non-determinism discussed above (Limitation 1), which we now know is severe enough to change which architecture a given metric favors, and, more subtly, by the possibility that our two ablations differ from our full method along dimensions we did not intend to isolate; we mitigated the latter by changing exactly one architectural axis per ablation (Section~\ref{sec:experiments}) and holding every hyperparameter in Appendix~\ref{secA1} fixed across all three variants, but a residual risk that some interaction effect confounds the equity and coverage findings cannot be fully excluded even at five seeds. On the curriculum, reduced-budget, and radiation-accessibility evaluations specifically (Limitation 5, three seeds rather than five), this threat is compounded further: we controlled for it by comparing only runs launched within the same script invocation at matched seeds, but Table~\ref{tab:hcmc-results} and Table~\ref{tab:curriculum-results} are not, strictly, evaluating identical trained checkpoints for a given seed across the two tables -- a residual source of noise beyond ordinary seed variance that the now-verified deterministic training configuration (Section~\ref{sec:experiments}) removes going forward, though it was not yet available when these specific tables were collected. \emph{External validity} -- whether findings on Xi'an and Ho Chi Minh City generalize elsewhere -- is threatened by exactly the two-city scope this paper is explicit about throughout (Limitation 2), and is the primary reason Section~\ref{sec1}'s contributions are framed as a research programme with Xi'an and Ho Chi Minh City as its first results rather than as claims already established across the full case-study set. \emph{Construct validity} -- whether our metrics actually measure what we intend them to -- is threatened by the coverage-for-accessibility substitution (Limitation 3), and here we have direct rather than only qualitative evidence: implementing the true radiation model for Ho Chi Minh City (Section~\ref{sec:experiments-radiation}) found it does \emph{not} reproduce the coverage proxy's architectural sensitivity, which is itself the threat materializing rather than merely a theoretical risk we flagged and moved past -- a reader should treat this paper's coverage-based comparisons as evidence about geographic spread specifically, not as a validated stand-in for population-weighted accessibility more generally. \emph{Conclusion validity} -- whether our statistical claims are properly hedged given the evidence -- is the threat we have tried hardest to manage explicitly throughout Section~\ref{sec:experiments}, by reporting standard deviations alongside every mean, by naming which between-variant differences we consider suggestive versus established, and by correcting, in this very manuscript's revision history, first an earlier single-seed draft that had overstated the full method's advantage before three seeds arrived, and later a three-seed equity finding that a five-seed sweep and a direct non-determinism check both showed did not hold up -- two corrections at increasing scale, which we read as the verification discipline working as intended rather than as evidence the discipline is failing.

\textbf{Practical implications for transit planners.} It is worth stating plainly what a transit planning authority could and could not reasonably do with a method like this one today, distinct from the more abstract scale and transfer-learning claims above. What it could do now: use the Tchebycheff weight-sampling mechanism (Section~\ref{sec:problem}) not to pick one weighting and commit to it, but to generate and compare several candidate expansion plans under explicitly different demand/equity/coverage priorities -- surfacing, concretely, what a more demand-focused plan would look like next to a more equity-focused one for the same city, budget, and existing network, which is a genuinely useful planning artifact even before the method's numbers are competitive with a specialized baseline on any single axis. What it could not yet reasonably do: replace an existing planning process's own demand-modeling and cost-estimation tools, or be trusted as the sole basis for an actual construction decision, given the honestly limited scope of validation reported in Section~\ref{sec:experiments} -- two cities, a coverage proxy that a direct check found does not track the true accessibility measure it stands in for, and training runs sensitive enough to non-determinism that a single run should not be over-trusted. We think this gap between ``useful exploratory tool today'' and ``validated decision-support system'' is exactly the right place for this paper to sit at this stage of the research programme, and exactly why Section~\ref{sec13}'s future-work items are framed as prerequisites to a stronger practical claim, not optional extensions of an already-sufficient one.

\textbf{Broader impact and ethical considerations.} A method that recommends where public infrastructure money gets spent is not a neutral piece of software, and it is worth stating directly what could go wrong if a method like this one were deployed carelessly, rather than leaving the reader to infer it. The equity objective in Section~\ref{sec:problem} is only as meaningful as the demographic groups it is computed over; in the Xi'an experiment (Section~\ref{sec:experiments}) those groups are price-based population bands inherited from the underlying dataset (Section~\ref{sec:data}), which is a reasonable proxy for socioeconomic disparity but not the only axis of transit inequity that matters -- age, disability, gender-differentiated travel patterns, and racial or ethnic segregation are all well-documented dimensions of transit equity \citep{raza2023} that a price-only grouping does not directly represent, and a planner using a system like this one should not mistake ``the equity metric improved'' for ``the network is now equitable in every sense that matters.'' There is also a more structural risk worth naming: an algorithmic recommendation, however well-intentioned its equity term, can function as a way to launder a politically contestable prioritization decision as an objective, technical output, obscuring the value judgments (which groups, which weighting, which time horizon) that went into it and that remain, properly, decisions for accountable public officials and the communities affected by them to make -- exactly the reason our Tchebycheff formulation supports a range of trade-offs rather than baking in one fixed weighting (Section~\ref{sec:problem}), and exactly why we think a tool like this should inform planning deliberation rather than substitute for it. We also note that the training-cost and carbon comparisons in Section~\ref{sec:experiments-carbon} are not incidental to this paper's contribution but part of it: a method's environmental cost during development is a real cost, however small relative to the infrastructure it ultimately helps plan, and reporting it honestly -- including the finding that our current implementation does not yet make efficient use of its GPU -- is consistent with treating that cost as something to be measured and minimized rather than assumed away, and with this paper's broader framing of network expansion as a resource-allocation problem in which the resources spent producing the recommendation are themselves part of the account.

\section{Conclusion}\label{sec13}

Metro network expansion is a decision cities get to make only rarely and can essentially never undo, which makes the case for bringing more rigorous, quantitative methods to it -- and for being honest about exactly what those methods have and have not yet demonstrated -- unusually strong. This paper proposed a GNN-based, multi-objective actor-critic policy for the Metro Network Expansion Problem that combines spatial representation learning \citep{su2024}, fairness-aware multi-objective reward decomposition \citep{zhang2024}, and a direct, architectural response to the training-efficiency critique raised by \citet{michailidis2026}, together with a rail-only scope discipline that lets the method extend, for the first time in this literature, to small and data-sparse emerging metro systems by treating each city's existing bus network as an auxiliary input signal rather than an additional design target.

We reported a first, fully real, GPU-trained experimental result on Xi'an, across five seeds per method (widened from an initial three specifically because the three-seed picture proved unreliable, below): a five-seed sweep finds no reliable advantage for our full method over either ablation on demand, equity, or coverage, reversing a three-seed equity finding this paper had earlier called its most suggestive result, which we retract explicitly rather than let stand once the fuller data contradicted it. In tracing that reversal, we found something we consider a more durable contribution than the retracted finding was: re-running the identical Xi'an configuration at seed 42 three separate times produced three different results, traced to two distinct causes -- non-deterministic GPU scatter-reduction in the shared GNN encoder, and a Gymnasium environment reset that only reseeds when explicitly asked to and otherwise silently draws from OS entropy -- a reproducibility hazard severe enough to invert which architecture a given metric appears to favor, and one we believe generalizes to other GNN-based MNEP methods built on the same default \texttt{torch\_geometric} operations or the same reset convention, not merely a quirk of our own implementation. We implemented and verified both fixes, confirming byte-identical re-runs, though this paper's own tables predate the fix and were not regenerated under it. A direct behavioral sanity check separately confirms the weight-conditioning mechanism that makes ``one policy, many trade-offs'' true rather than aspirational for at least some trained instances, after we caught and fixed a bug that had silently made it false in an earlier, otherwise-unremarkable-looking training run -- though two of five Xi'an seeds now show the mechanism collapsing to weight-insensitive behavior after training, a pattern that also appears on Ho Chi Minh City's graph (below) and that we report as a genuine, if incompletely understood, training-dynamics limitation rather than smooth over. On the narrower question of raw demand satisfaction under a matched, modest training budget, the tabular RL baseline of \citet{michailidis2026} outperforms all three of our variants on Xi'an, corroborated by a second, independent line of evidence from real measured training-cost data (Section~\ref{sec:experiments-carbon}) -- a result we report plainly rather than obscure, and read as consistent with rather than contradicting their central claim at this network's scale.

We also reported a second, structurally distinct city, Ho Chi Minh City, whose irregular polygon graph exercised the method's action-space generalization for the first time and surfaced a third implementation pitfall worth naming alongside the weight-conditioning bug and the Tchebycheff-scalarization bug: a greedy-evaluation protocol valid on Xi'an's grid silently produced degenerate zero-demand results on Ho Chi Minh City's graph, fixed by evaluating from multiple random starting zones rather than one fixed one -- and, per the reproducibility finding above, the GPU non-determinism itself is a fourth. There, the GNN encoder's contribution was starker than on Xi'an -- an encoder ablation collapsed to near-zero demand where the full method did not -- and, with Xi'an now available as a trained predecessor, we ran the first measured test of the cross-city curriculum this paper motivates: warm-starting Ho Chi Minh City's policy from Xi'an's trained weights produced non-zero demand at the very first, pre-training episode where a random initialization produced none, direct evidence of genuine cross-city transfer. A matched-budget comparison after continued training did not establish a final-performance advantage, but re-testing under a reduced episode budget -- the actual sample-efficiency claim the curriculum is motivated by, and a test we said in an earlier draft we had not yet run -- found warm-starting reaching non-trivial demand and consistently higher coverage than training from scratch in most seeds, direct if imperfect evidence for that claim. We additionally implemented the true radiation-model accessibility measure for Ho Chi Minh City specifically, the one city where real population and coordinate data make it computable, and found it does not share the coverage proxy's sensitivity to architecture that this paper otherwise reports -- a genuine, if modest, construct-validity finding we report as a null result rather than the confirmatory check we had expected. Producing that result surfaced a fifth implementation pitfall: a checkpoint-saving routine correctly scoped to exclude a city-specific input layer from cross-city transfer silently omitted that same layer from same-city post-hoc evaluation too, leaving a trained model's input projection at a random initialization whenever a saved checkpoint was reloaded for evaluation rather than continued training; we caught it, fixed it, retrained the affected checkpoints, and confirmed the qualitative null-result finding survives the correction unchanged.

\subsection*{Future work}

That reading generates a concrete, near-term roadmap rather than an open-ended one, and we state it explicitly rather than leave it implicit -- shorter than an earlier version of this list, since widening the seed sweep, testing the reduced-budget curriculum, implementing the radiation model for Ho Chi Minh City, and enabling and verifying deterministic training are no longer future work but results reported above. First, and most immediately given how much it mattered for the findings already in this paper: extend the deterministic regeneration already completed for Table~\ref{tab:results} (Section~\ref{sec:experiments}) to the remaining tables (Table~\ref{tab:weight-sensitivity} onward, including Ho Chi Minh City's Table~\ref{tab:hcmc-results}), to determine whether those architectural comparisons are stable under deterministic training or remain genuinely inconclusive at this sample size even once run-to-run noise is removed -- deterministic training is now confirmed to produce byte-identical re-runs and, on Xi'an's main table, took under half an hour on CPU alone for the full 15-run sweep, so the remaining regeneration is a matter of time and careful re-verification (of figures and of Ho Chi Minh City's longer per-run training time) rather than any remaining technical uncertainty. Second: run the same full method, unmodified, on Chengdu -- the single experiment this paper's central scale-dependent hypothesis most directly hinges on, and the one most likely to either vindicate or meaningfully qualify \citeauthor{michailidis2026}'s efficiency claim outside the mid-sized regime it has so far been tested in. Third: widen the curriculum, reduced-budget-curriculum, and radiation-accessibility evaluations (Sections~\ref{sec:experiments-radiation}--\ref{sec:experiments-curriculum}) from three seeds to five, matching the two cities' main ablation tables, to determine whether the reduced-budget curriculum's seed-42 demand exception (Section~\ref{sec:experiments-curriculum}) is a stable minority pattern or noise. Fourth: investigate why the radiation-model accessibility measure showed no architectural sensitivity where the coverage proxy did (Section~\ref{sec:experiments-radiation}) -- whether by testing at a higher station-budget-to-candidate-region ratio than Ho Chi Minh City's 20-of-322, or by incorporating the radiation model into the training reward directly rather than evaluating it only post-hoc, which this submission's scope did not allow. Fifth: extend Hanoi's data pipeline and run it as the fourth and final case-study city, completing the four-city design (Section~\ref{sec:data}). None of these five steps requires a new architecture or a new formulation -- the method, the code, and the data pipelines described in this paper already support all five; what remains is running them, and we intend to.

\subsection{Ho Chi Minh City (ablations and cross-city curriculum)}

Ho Chi Minh City results (Sections~\ref{sec:experiments-hcmc}--\ref{sec:experiments-curriculum}, \texttt{scripts/train\_hcmc.py}) use the same hyperparameters as Table~\ref{tab:hparams-ours}, with the following differences reflecting Ho Chi Minh City's irregular, variable-degree candidate-region graph (322 zones, 1{,}914 contiguity edges, 13{,}042 OD edges) rather than Xi'an's uniform grid: the action space is the contiguous-neighbor realization of Table~\ref{tab:action-space} rather than the 8-directional grid walk, so per-step action counts vary by node degree instead of being capped at 8; greedy evaluation averages over 5 random starting zones per weight setting (\texttt{greedy\_eval}, Section~\ref{sec:experiments-hcmc}) rather than one fixed zone; and the equity objective is computed over 4 income brackets derived from the CSL\_HCMC dataset's \texttt{res\_income\_1}--\texttt{4} fields (Section~\ref{sec:data}) rather than Xi'an's 5 price-based groups. The \texttt{curriculum} condition (Section~\ref{sec:experiments-curriculum}) additionally loads the correspondingly-seeded Xi'an \texttt{full} checkpoint's policy, value-head, weight-embedding, and GNN message-passing-layer parameters (\texttt{--init\_from\_xian}) before training begins, with every other hyperparameter unchanged from the \texttt{scratch} condition. Random seeds: 1, 2, 3, 7, 42 for the ablation table (Table~\ref{tab:hcmc-results}, matching Table~\ref{tab:results}'s 5-seed sweep); 1, 2, 42 for the curriculum, reduced-budget curriculum, and radiation-accessibility evaluations (Tables~\ref{tab:curriculum-results}, \ref{tab:reduced-budget-curriculum}, \ref{tab:radiation-accessibility}), matched to the three Xi'an \texttt{full} checkpoints saved for warm-starting.

\subsection{Radiation-model accessibility (Ho Chi Minh City only)}

The post-hoc radiation-accessibility evaluation (Section~\ref{sec:experiments-radiation}, \texttt{scripts/eval\_hcmc\_radiation.py}) loads a saved Ho Chi Minh City checkpoint, rebuilds the identical graph and reward objects \texttt{scripts/train\_hcmc.py} uses, and reuses the same argmax greedy rollout over 5 random starting zones, so the only new computation is \texttt{src/data/hcmc.py}'s \texttt{build\_radiation\_probabilities} (the $n \times n$ flow-probability matrix from each zone's real \texttt{Pop\_2019} and projected \texttt{x\_centroid}/\texttt{y\_centroid}, $O(n^2 \log n)$ for $n = 322$, well under a second) and \texttt{radiation\_accessibility\_score} (the closest-built-station aggregation described in Section~\ref{sec:experiments-radiation}). Both are unit-tested against a hand-computed three-zone synthetic example in \texttt{tests/test\_hcmc\_data.py}, including a check that the resulting probability matrix is correctly asymmetric ($p_{ij} \neq p_{ji}$ in general, since the radiation model's normalization depends on the origin zone's own population) and that the aggregate accessibility score is monotonically non-decreasing as more stations are added to the built set.
